The Pattern Is the Answer

Ten years without a playoff game is no longer a roster problem in Detroit. It is a foundation problem.

April 2026 24 min read Detroit Red Wings
Contents
  1. I. The Ownership Temperature
  2. II. What Happened to “That” Steve Yzerman?
  3. III. A Philosophy Without an Identity
  4. IV. The DNA of This Roster
  5. V. The Coaching Carousel
  6. VI. Call to Action

Let’s just say it plainly. The Detroit Red Wings have missed the Stanley Cup Playoffs ten consecutive times. Ten. In their centennial season, their 100th year as a franchise, they completed a decade of organizational history that will be remembered, at best, as forgettable.

They have fired multiple coaches, drafted high, spent in free agency, and hired one of the most respected names in the sport to run their front office. They have handed their franchise player a long-term deal and asked him to carry a culture that, for reasons this fanbase deserves a real answer to, refuses to be carried.

So here we are again. Detroit now holds the record for the longest active playoff drought in the NHL, now that Buffalo has gotten themselves back in. After another familiar late-season collapse, the honest question is no longer what went wrong with the roster. It’s what is wrong with this organization at its foundation.

Because at some point, the pattern is the answer.

However, before we go further, something needs to be said plainly: this team is better than it was the day Yzerman arrived. The core is legitimate. The defensive group has improved. There are prospects in the system with real upside, and there are genuine reasons to believe in the direction of this roster if you focus on particular aspects and things. That is true and it deserves acknowledgment. But progress and results are not the same thing. And after ten years without a single playoff game, the standard for this franchise cannot be “better than we were.” It has to be more than that. You can recognize improvement and still demand accountability for why a decade has passed without the most basic benchmark of NHL success being met.

Both things are true at the same time, and this piece holds both.

Section IThe Ownership Temperature

Culture in any organization flows from the top. Not from the coach’s whiteboard. Not from the captain’s pregame speech. From the top. And the best players in the world, whatever their individual priorities, want to play for ownership that is genuinely, fully invested. Not just financially. Fully.

Detroit had that once. Mike Ilitch (God rest his soul) had a relationship with this franchise that was personal, relentless, and deeply felt. If you want to understand what that looked like in practice, go find Jimmy Devellano’s “The Road to Hockeytown,” a book from 2008, and read about the relationship between “Jimmy D” and “Mr. I.” The trust. The urgency. The alignment between ownership and hockey operations that allowed them to build something generational.

Then ask yourself honestly: does anything resembling that relationship appear to exist between Steve Yzerman and Chris Ilitch today? Most of us land in the same place on that question. Chris Ilitch has funded this rebuild. He has been patient. What he has not done, at any visible moment, is create the kind of ownership urgency that separates franchises who expect to win from franchises who merely hope to. Look at Bill Foley in Vegas, who built a culture of genuine excellence in a market that wasn’t supposed to care about hockey and now draws free agents the way Detroit used to. Look at what Ryan Smith is quietly constructing in Utah, an owner who is in the fire with his organization, invests heavily, demands results, and is humble enough to surround himself with people who know more than he does about hockey.

There is a difference between an owner who demands excellence and one who finances it and steps back.

When was the last time you saw a genuine moment of accountability from the top of this organization? Not a press conference where Yzerman spoke in careful, measured generalities. Not a prepared statement. A real moment, on the record, where the standard was raised and someone was held to it. If you’re struggling to answer that, it’s not a coincidence. That is the temperature of the building on Woodward Avenue.

And the building itself tells part of the story. There is still a fenced-in dirt lot out front of Little Caesars Arena nearly ten years after it opened. “The District Detroit” was supposed to be a transformational development project that became one of the more high-profile broken promises in this city’s recent history. For a franchise with eleven Stanley Cup championships and one of the most loyal fanbases in North American professional sports, that absence of public accountability, on the ice and off it, is its own statement.

Patience from ownership is a virtue in a rebuild. Passivity is not. And from the outside looking in, it has become increasingly difficult to tell the difference in Detroit.

Section IIWhat Happened to “That” Steve Yzerman?

In 2018 and 2019, Steve Yzerman’s reputation in hockey operations was about as bulletproof as it gets. Tampa Bay was, and still is, a powerhouse in the Eastern Conference. A juggernaut built with intention: high-octane offense, physically dominant defensemen, elite goaltending, and an organizational identity that ran from the ownership box through the locker room. The Lightning knew exactly what kind of team they were building, why they were building it that way, and how every move connected to the next.

Detroit fans were right to be excited when he came home. That excitement was earned by what “The Captain” had built in Tampa Bay. People in Hockeytown want to know where the guy who drafted Nikita Kucherov in the second round is. People want to know where the guy who turned an aging Ben Bishop into Erik Cernak went. People are wondering where the guy who took a disgruntled Drouin and turned him into an incredibly useful Sergachev went.

But here’s the thing: all those Tampa transactions were nearly a decade ago, or longer in some cases. At some point, specifically right now, going into year eight, Detroit fans are owed results from this version of Steve Yzerman, not the Tampa Bay version. Those are two different people at this point. Protecting the current GM with the legacy résumé of the one who left Tampa is how bad management gets a pass for too long. The name hasn’t changed. The track record we are actually living with has.

We hired the architect of Tampa’s powerhouse. Seven years later, we’re still waiting for him to break ground.

The honest question isn’t whether Yzerman is a good GM in the abstract. It’s whether the genius we saw in Tampa was a product of Yzerman operating within a culture that Jeff Vinik’s ownership and a pre-existing foundation made possible, and whether dropping him into Detroit’s organizational climate has quietly neutralized exactly what made him great in the first place. That is not an excuse for him. It is a sharper version of the indictment. If the environment is the problem, it is still his problem to fix. He has the title. He has the authority. Seven years in, the environment cannot still be the answer.

Section IIIA Philosophy Without an Identity

Every franchise that wins consistently believes in something specific. Tampa believes in speed, structure, and protecting its core at almost any cost. Carolina believes in analytics, defensive accountability, and building sustainable depth over star-driven rosters. Boston believes in a certain kind of hardness, a winning DNA they actively recruit for and work to protect. There is no single right answer on organizational philosophy. But there has to be an answer, a consistent one that everyone inside the building understands and is held to, whether it is October or March.

What does Detroit believe in? Spend a few honest minutes trying to answer that and you will feel the problem. On the draft side, Yzerman does appear to have a type, and in certain cases that has worked. The free agent targets, however, have felt reactive rather than vision-driven. The trade deadline behavior has oscillated between aggressive and passive with no evident connective logic across years. You cannot complete the sentence “Detroit builds teams by…” without immediately producing a counterexample from somewhere in the last five seasons.

And the draft record reinforces the point. Detroit has not drafted a 100-point player since Fedorov in 1989, and has not drafted a 90-point player since Zetterberg in 1999. For anyone born after 1991, there has been exactly one player drafted by this organization who reached the 90-point threshold in their entire lifetime. That is not a scouting anomaly. That is a reflection of what this organization has consistently prioritized and consistently failed to produce at the highest offensive level.

Nothing illustrates the culture gap more starkly than a detail that came out of Todd McLellan’s postgame press conference on Sergei Fedorov’s retirement night. McLellan mentioned that one of the younger players on the team, a drafted Red Wing and member of this organization, looked at Vladimir Konstantinov during the ceremony and asked: “Who is the guy in the wheelchair?”

Read that again.

Vladimir Konstantinov. One of the most beloved figures in Red Wings history. A man whose career was cut short by a tragedy that shook this entire city and galvanized one of the most emotionally significant Stanley Cup celebrations in NHL history. And a player wearing a Detroit Red Wings jersey did not know who he was.

That is not a small detail. That is a flashing red light. If you are not actively creating a sense of pride and belonging in this identity, if these players do not walk into that building every day understanding what came before them and what it demands of them, then you do not have a culture. You simply have a collection of employees reporting for work in a place where giants and legends once stood.

Do you want Little Caesars Arena and Detroit, Michigan to be a premier destination for NHL players? Or do you want it to just be a fancy hockey museum?

Section IVThe DNA of This Roster

Before anything else: these are human beings. Real people living real lives, and by any objective standard, fortunate ones. Hockey players are as team-first as any athletes in professional sports, and this roster is not short on character in the personal sense. That matters and it should be said. But character and competitive DNA are not the same thing. And this roster has a problem with the latter that has been visible and consistent for years.

The problem is at forward, beyond the top line. And it is not a subtle one.

Take Andrew Copp, JT Compher, and Michael Rasmussen. Three centers who have occupied significant roster space and salary cap real estate behind Dylan Larkin for multiple seasons now. Since 2022-23, these three players have combined for 799 games in a Detroit uniform. In that span, one of the three went four or more consecutive games without a point on 62 separate occasions. Sixty-two. Four games without a point represents roughly 5% of an NHL season. For some perspective, 5% of an NFL season is approximately 83% of a single game, about 50 minutes of football.

That is not a minor gap in production. That is a structural failure of roster construction, and it has been allowed to continue while the core’s window quietly ages around it. You have a first-line center who deserves real support, and you are running two key roster spots at a combined 0.42 points per game for three straight seasons. Where is the urgency? Where is the accountability for that specific failure?

It is also worth noting that the Detroit Red Wings have had no Director of Pro Scouting since Mark Howe retired in 2021. Not every organization structures the role identically, but when the person responsible for evaluating NHL-level talent is undefined, responsibilities blur, communication deteriorates, and decisions get made without the institutional rigor that position demands. This is not a minor organizational gap. It is the department responsible for identifying which players to acquire and which to move on from.

There have been useful additions. But the two most impactful pro acquisitions of the Yzerman era required almost no proactive effort to land. DeBrincat recruited himself home. Kane came because DeBrincat made the call. That is not a sustainable acquisition model for a contender. At some point the front office has to go get the right people rather than wait for them to arrive.

Section VThe Coaching Carousel Was Never the Real Problem

Jeff Blashill. Derek Lalonde. Todd McLellan. Three very different coaches with three different philosophies, communication styles, and approaches to accountability. The same result. McLellan is clearly the best of the three, and it is not particularly close. The team has responded to his directness in ways they never consistently did for his predecessors. But after that October run, Detroit could not string together more than four consecutive wins the rest of the way.

The same McLellan. The same accountability. The same structure. What changed? The roster did not change. And that is the point.

If Lalonde was considered too lenient and the room did not respond to that consistently, and McLellan is direct, demanding, and publicly honest to a degree that is rare in this league, and some of those same players still cannot sustain a response to that, what is the common thread? It is not the head coach. It is who is in the room.

Firing coaches is what organizations do when they want to appear decisive without actually changing anything structural. It produces a visible action that ownership and management can point to without requiring honest examination of the foundation those coaches were given. Different coach. Same building. Same result in April.

Section VICall to Action

This is not a call to blow everything up. That is the easy thing to demand from the outside and it almost never fixes the actual problem. The real ask is harder than that. It is for the people inside that building to drop the organizational ego, sit with the discomfort of what ten consecutive years is telling them, and be genuinely honest about what is broken and why. Not publicly. Not in a press conference. Actually honest, behind closed doors, where the real work either gets done or it doesn’t.

There is enough blame to go around and it should be distributed honestly. The largest share belongs at the top, because culture is set from ownership down. A meaningful portion belongs to Yzerman, who is the GM and is directly accountable for player identification, staff structure, and the hockey operations culture he has either built or permitted. And yes, some of it goes on the players. Detroit is a city that was built on adversity, that grinds through things other places quit on.

Ten years. Multiple coaches. An Olympic gold medalist in Larkin whose prime is disappearing one missed April at a time. A front office operating on borrowed reputation. A building where a drafted Red Wing can stand on the ice on Fedorov’s retirement night and not know who Vladimir Konstantinov is.

At what point do we stop asking what’s wrong with the team, and start asking what kind of organization keeps producing this pattern, and what it would actually take to change it?

The culture doesn’t lie. It never does, never has, and never will. It just waits for you to stop making excuses for it.
