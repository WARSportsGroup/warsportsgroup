**Purpose.** This is not a plan for how to run Red Wings hockey operations. That responsibility belongs to the organization's leadership. It is a set of ideas and questions, offered in the spirit of an owner's stated goal: to operate unlike any other team in hockey.

## Executive Summary

**The opportunity.** Every NHL team can buy the same data, hire analysts and scout Europe. What no team can buy is the speed at which an organization turns a question into a tested answer, and a tested answer into a better decision. This report proposes that Detroit build that capability deliberately, and become the NHL organization that learns about hockey faster than anyone else.

**Why Detroit, why now.** Chris Ilitch has said the Red Wings have been at their very best when operating unlike any other team in hockey, and that the modern game rewards organizations that evolve continuously. The organization is choosing a new leader of hockey operations. And Detroit's greatest innovations, from the Production Line to the Russian Five, the left-wing lock and late-round European scouting, all came from the same habit: going looking where others were not.

**Why it can work.** Carolina won the 2026 Stanley Cup with a general manager trained as a research scientist. Outside scientific thinking already wins. And while the hard cap limits what teams spend on players, spending on hockey operations is not capped. Knowledge is one of the few places a committed ownership group can still outinvest rivals.

**Where the edge lives.** Tactics are copied within an offseason. The lasting advantage is in work rivals cannot watch: how players are developed, how prospects are projected, how health and aging are managed, and how decisions are made and reviewed.

**The operating system.** Every initiative feeds one loop: a question bank, research projects, honestly graded findings, testing in Toledo and Grand Rapids, decisions with written predictions, and scheduled reviews of what actually happened. Success is measured by cycle time, adoption, calibration, beliefs retired and the talent Detroit attracts.

**The eleven initiatives.**

| # | Initiative | What it does | When |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Decision Ledger | Records predictions for major decisions and reviews them against outcomes | Year 1 |
| 2 | Toledo and Grand Rapids Proving Ground | Tests methods and systems in the ECHL first, then the AHL, before they reach Detroit | Year 1 |
| 3 | Detroit Scouting Network | Finds the most accurate evaluators through an open, scored forecasting competition | Year 1 |
| 4 | Concepts Over Positions | Pilots a system built on shared concepts instead of fixed positions | Year 1 pilot, once researched |
| 5 | Goalie Bullpen | Builds several viable goaltenders instead of betting everything on one true number one | Year 1 |
| 6 | Player Experience Office | Makes being a Red Wing, for players and families, a measured advantage | Year 1 |
| 7 | Detroit Hockey Fellowship | Brings senior experts from other fields, starting with soccer, to study hockey | Years 1 to 3 |
| 8 | Player Data Partnership | Earns player trust by using data to help players, never against them | Years 1 to 3 |
| 9 | Innovation Partner Program | Partners with Michigan universities and companies to co-fund research | Years 1 to 3 |
| 10 | Detroit Hockey Science Institute | Studies how players learn and why skills transfer, built in phases, with a public conference as its outward face | Years 1 to 10 |
| 11 | Centers of Excellence | Builds a long-term development and coach education network | Years 3 to 10 |

**What it costs.** Year one costs roughly $1.4 million to $3.5 million and needs no new building. A mature program in year ten could cost roughly $14 million to $36 million a year, plus about $27 million to $56 million in cumulative capital, most of it for the Institute. All figures are estimates. Capital is committed only after earlier phases prove their value.

**How Detroit would know it is failing.** Every initiative carries advance kill criteria. Research that changes no decisions is stopped. No building is funded without proof. Any breach of player data protections ends that program immediately.

**The ask.** Not a budget. Consideration of these ideas, and the questions in Part V, as the Red Wings choose their next leader of hockey operations.

The lesson of Detroit's history is not what the Red Wings discovered. It is that they were willing to go looking.

## Part I: Why Now

### The standard ownership has already set

On September 14, 2026, Chris Ilitch sat down with Bob Wojnowski of The Detroit News for a 45-minute interview, his first public comments after a difficult summer. Asked about the search for a new hockey operations leader, he said he will hire when he finds the right person: an innovative difference-maker who can build a championship-caliber organization. [1]

In the same conversation, right after describing the leader he wants, he backed the idea with history. Innovation, he said, played a central role in Detroit's Stanley Cups. He named the Production Line and what it combined: Sid Abel's playmaking, Ted Lindsay's checking and grit, and Gordie Howe's generational blend of skill and physicality. He named the Russian Five, the left-wing lock, drafting behind the Iron Curtain and redefining late-round European scouting. [1]

Later, asked whether he wants an analytics-driven model like the one Scott Harris brought to the Tigers, he went further. The Red Wings, he said, have been at their very best historically when "operating unlike any other team in hockey," innovating ahead of their competition. [1]

In that same answer, he said analytics are a big part of it, but sports science, player development and injury prevention matter too. The modern game, in his view, rewards organizations that evolve continuously. [1]

Two other answers point in the same direction. Asked about hiring from outside the organization, Ilitch said he sees merit in bringing in experience, skills and abilities that may not exist inside the Red Wings today. And he described interim GM Shawn Horcoff as a student of the game and a continuous learner who does not lean only on his playing experience. [1]

Taken together, ownership has described an organization that looks outward for new knowledge, keeps learning and evolves continuously. That is close to the thesis of this report.

This report takes that standard seriously and asks a practical question. If operating unlike any other team is the goal, what would that actually look like in 2027 and beyond?

### Why this moment matters

The Red Wings are searching for a new leader of hockey operations, with Shawn Horcoff serving as interim general manager and remaining a candidate for the permanent job. [1] Ilitch has said the next president of hockey operations will set the staff and carry out the plan, with full authority to do so. [1]

That shapes what this report is and is not. It is not a plan for how to run the Red Wings. That belongs to whoever gets the job. It is a set of ideas and questions worth putting in front of that person, offered while the plan is still being written.

Detroit has won eleven Stanley Cups. This report offers eleven initiatives aimed at earning the next one.

### What every team already has

The honest starting point is that the obvious advantages are gone.

Every NHL team buys tracking data. Every team has an analytics department. Every team scouts Europe. A 2023 survey of all 32 front offices found an average hockey operations staff of 34 people, including about three analytics staffers at a typical club. [2] Good ideas still matter, but visible ideas get copied within an offseason.

So the next edge is unlikely to come from doing the standard things slightly better. It has to come from somewhere others are not looking yet, which is exactly what Detroit did in each of the examples Ilitch named.

### The one place a well-resourced owner can outspend rivals

The NHL's hard cap limits what teams spend on player salaries. It does not limit what they spend on hockey operations. ESPN described this in 2022 as the league's quiet arms race: staff, travel, technology and research, with a wide gap between the haves and the have-nots. [3]

That creates a real strategic choice. Every dollar spent on players is constrained by the same ceiling for all 32 teams. Dollars spent on knowledge, people and the systems that turn research into decisions are not. For an ownership group willing to invest, that is where a durable advantage can be built.

It is also where a risk sits, and this report does not hide it. Leagues can change the rules. The CFL introduced a cap on football operations spending in 2019. [4] That is a reason to invest in things that last, like knowledge, partnerships and habits, rather than headcount alone.

### Proof that this works, and a humility check

The clearest evidence that outside scientific thinking wins in today's NHL is the current champion.

Carolina won the 2026 Stanley Cup. [5] Its general manager, Eric Tulsky, holds a chemistry PhD from Berkeley, spent twelve years managing nanotechnology research teams and holds 27 U.S. patents. He joined the Hurricanes as a consultant in 2014. [6] Front Office Sports reports that Carolina's front office has hired data engineers, neuroscientists and software developers. [7] They also carried three goaltenders for much of the season, and the least proven of them led the team in starts and wins. [55]

That cuts two ways. It proves the approach can win a Cup. It also means Detroit would not be first. The question this report asks is not whether Detroit can copy Carolina. It is whether Detroit can go further: turning outside thinking from a few smart hires into a permanent system for learning about hockey faster than anyone else.

## Part II: Detroit Went Looking

The four innovations below look different on the surface. One is a line combination, one is a group of players, one is a tactical system and one is a scouting strategy. Underneath, they share a root. In each case, Detroit saw something other teams were not looking for.

### 1. The Production Line: seeing how the pieces fit

In the late 1940s, coach Tommy Ivan put a veteran center, Sid Abel, between two young wingers, Ted Lindsay and Gordie Howe. Abel's legs were slowing. Ivan's bet was that two fast wingers could cover for that and bring out the best of what Abel had left. [8]

The media named the trio after the thing Detroit did better than anyone: building on an assembly line. [9] In 1949-50, the Production Line finished first, second and third in NHL scoring, a feat the Red Wings note has never been matched. [10]

The lesson is not about talent alone. All three players were great. The innovation was seeing that the combination would be greater than the parts.

### 2. The Russian Five: courage over consensus

In 1989, drafting Soviet players carried enormous risk. There was no guarantee they would ever be allowed to leave. Jim Devellano later described the strategy plainly: "We would draft the best players, and if they happened to be behind the Iron Curtain, we would use our ownership resources to find a way to get them out." [11]

Detroit took Sergei Fedorov in the fourth round (74th overall) and Vladimir Konstantinov in the eleventh (221st overall). [12] Scotty Bowman first put all five Russians on the ice together on October 27, 1995, in Calgary. Two seasons later, with that group at the core, Detroit won its first Stanley Cup in 42 years. [13]

The lesson here is ownership-level risk tolerance. Most teams saw an obstacle. Detroit saw an opportunity others were too cautious to take.

### 3. The left-wing lock: importing ideas from outside the NHL

Detroit did not invent the left-wing lock. Its roots appear to trace back to Czechoslovakia. [14]

What Detroit did was go find it. Assistant coach Barry Smith has described traveling to Sweden to study systems he found interesting, because the Wings of the early 1990s could win high-scoring games but struggled to win tight ones. [14] Smith worked the system out with Scotty Bowman, and the coaching staff brought it into training camp for the 1994-95 season. [14]

This is the example most relevant to the rest of this report. It is not knowledge about a player. It is knowledge about hockey itself, imported from outside the league by someone curious enough to look.

### 4. European scouting: looking where others weren't

Nicklas Lidström was playing irregularly for Västerås in Sweden in 1988-89. Detroit had to work harder than other teams to scout him properly, and took him 53rd overall. [12] NHL.com later described that draft as the template for a generation of European finds, including Tomas Holmström, Pavel Datsyuk, Henrik Zetterberg, Jonathan Ericsson, Johan Franzen and Gustav Nyquist. [12]

European scouting director Håkan Andersson found Datsyuk and Zetterberg while watching other players. Datsyuk went 171st overall in 1998. Zetterberg went 210th in 1999. [15]

The lesson is that the market misses things. Detroit built a process for finding what others overlooked, and it paid off for two decades.

### The common root, and why nostalgia is the wrong response

| Anchor | Type of innovation | Modern equivalent in this report |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Production Line | Seeing how pieces fit | Hockey Science Institute, Concepts Over Positions |
| The Russian Five | Courage over consensus | Toledo and Grand Rapids Proving Ground, Player Experience Office |
| The left-wing lock | Importing ideas from outside | Detroit Hockey Fellowship |
| European scouting | Looking where others weren't | Detroit Scouting Network, Decision Ledger |

It would be easy to read this history and conclude that Detroit should find the next Datsyuk or the next Russian Five. That is the wrong lesson.

The frontiers Detroit explored in 1989 are no longer frontiers. Every team scouts Europe. Every team studies systems on video within days. The places that were hidden then are fully mapped now.

The lesson that still applies is not what Detroit found. It is that Detroit was willing to go looking. The question for today is where the unexplored territory is now, and whether the organization is built to go find it.

## Part III: The Thesis

### Learn faster than everyone else

Every NHL team can buy the same data. What no team can buy is the speed at which an organization turns a question into a tested answer, and a tested answer into a better decision.

That is the thesis of this report. The idea itself is not new. Business writers such as Arie de Geus and Peter Senge argued decades ago that an organization's ability to learn faster than its competitors may be its most durable advantage. What is new is applying it deliberately to hockey operations, in a capped league where knowledge is one of the few things money can still buy.

### Where the edge actually lives

Not all knowledge is equally valuable, because not all knowledge stays private.

Tactics are visible. A new forecheck shows up on video the next morning, and 31 teams can study it. Carolina's possession-driven approach is a good example: Front Office Sports reports it has since been widely copied across the league. [7]

Other knowledge is much harder to see from outside:

- **How a team develops its own players.** Practice design, skill coaching and individual development plans happen behind closed doors.
- **How it projects prospects.** Which traits it trusts, and how well its scouts' confidence matches reality.
- **How it manages health and aging.** When skating declines, what prevents injury, what extends careers.
- **How it makes decisions.** Whether it learns from its mistakes or repeats them.

That is where this report aims. Detroit should still pursue tactical ideas, but the lasting advantage is in the work rivals cannot watch.

### Knowledge is useless until someone acts on it

More data is not the goal. At the 2026 Linköping Hockey Analytics Conference, one presenter described walking an assistant coach through the numbers for 45 minutes, only to learn the coach did not know what he was supposed to do differently. [16]

That story is the central problem. Research that never changes a decision is an expensive hobby. So the eleven initiatives in this report are built around a single operating loop, and every one of them has a job inside it.

1. **Question bank.** A living list of unanswered hockey questions, ranked by how much the answer would change decisions, how testable it is and how uncertain Detroit is today.
2. **Research project.** A small team from different disciplines takes on one question, for six weeks or five years.
3. **Graded finding.** Every result is labeled honestly: proven, promising, or cannot be measured yet. "We can't measure this yet" is still knowledge.
4. **Proving Ground test.** Promising findings are tried in Toledo and Grand Rapids before they reach the NHL roster.
5. **Decision with a written prediction.** Major decisions record what Detroit expects to happen and how confident it is.
6. **Outcome review.** On a set schedule, predictions are compared with results. What was learned goes back into the question bank.

Each turn of the loop produces either a better decision or a retired belief. Both count.

### Detroit already knows how to run this loop

The Production Line was named after Detroit's assembly lines for a reason. [9] This region built the modern auto industry by treating improvement as a system: design, test, build, measure, fix, repeat. Automakers do not wait for a crisis to start research. They run it continuously because steady discovery compounds.

That culture still exists here in concrete form. Wayne State University's work on head injury tolerance, developed for the auto industry in 1960, remains a foundation for motor vehicle and sports equipment safety standards, according to the university. [17] Detroit does not need to import an engineering mindset. It needs to point the one it already has at hockey.

### How learning velocity is measured

A thesis ownership cannot measure is just a slogan. These five measures make it real:

| Measure | What it tells ownership |
| --- | --- |
| Cycle time | How long it takes a question to become a usable answer |
| Adoption rate | The share of findings that actually changed a decision or a practice |
| Calibration | Whether the confidence of scouts and models matches real outcomes |
| Beliefs retired | How often the organization formally admits it was wrong |
| Talent pull | Whether strong researchers, coaches and scouts want to come to Detroit |

The rest of this report describes the eleven initiatives that feed this loop, what each would cost, and how Detroit would know if one is failing.

## Part IV: The Eleven Initiatives

Each initiative below has one job inside the learning loop. Some are cheap and could start this season. Some are long bets that should only be funded after earlier work proves itself. None of them is meant to replace the judgment of the people running hockey operations. They are meant to give those people better questions, better evidence and a faster way to learn from results.

| # | Initiative | Role in the loop | When | Cost level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Decision Ledger | Record decisions and learn from outcomes | Year 1 | Low |
| 2 | Toledo and Grand Rapids Proving Ground | Test ideas before they reach the NHL | Year 1 | Low |
| 3 | Detroit Scouting Network | Widen and sharpen evaluation | Year 1 | Low to medium |
| 4 | Concepts Over Positions | Build knowledge about how the game is played | Year 1 pilot, once researched | Low |
| 5 | Goalie Bullpen | Turn goaltending into a depth chart instead of a bet | Year 1 | Low to medium |
| 6 | Player Experience Office | Retain and attract players | Year 1 | Low |
| 7 | Detroit Hockey Fellowship | Import outside knowledge | Years 1 to 3 | Medium |
| 8 | Player Data Partnership | Earn player trust and better data | Years 1 to 3 | Medium |
| 9 | Innovation Partner Program | Fund and extend research | Years 1 to 3 | Offsets cost |
| 10 | Detroit Hockey Science Institute | Create new knowledge and make Detroit a destination for it | Years 1 to 10, phased | Medium, then high |
| 11 | Centers of Excellence | Widen the network Detroit learns from | Years 3 to 10 | High |

Every initiative follows the same structure: what it is, why Detroit, what already exists elsewhere, what would be new, the strongest objections, a rough cost, the first twelve months, how success is measured, and when Detroit should shut it down. That last part matters. An idea without kill criteria is a wish, not a plan.

*All cost figures in this part are the author's estimates unless a source is cited, and they should be treated as ranges for discussion, not budgets.*

### Initiative 1: The Decision Ledger

**In one sentence:** every major hockey decision gets written down with a prediction and a confidence level, then reviewed honestly against what actually happened.

**What it is.** Before a draft pick, trade, signing, extension or development plan, the decision-makers record three things: what they expect to happen, how confident they are, and why. On a set schedule, at one, three and five years after the decision, the organization compares those predictions with reality. Over time, Detroit learns which of its scouts, models and instincts are well calibrated, and which are not.

Two components are built in from the start:

- **A calibration audit of Detroit's scouting history.** The Red Wings have decades of written scouting reports. Comparing what scouts projected with how careers actually unfolded is one of the cheapest, highest-value research projects available to this team right now.
- **A red team for major decisions.** Before a significant trade or signing, one person is assigned to make the strongest case against it. The point is not to block decisions. It is to make sure the organization has heard the best counterargument before committing.

**Why Detroit.** Ilitch described running an organizational performance review of each area after last season's spring slide. [1] The Decision Ledger turns that kind of review from a one-time event into a permanent habit.

**What already exists.** Forecasting research shows this approach works. In a four-year U.S. intelligence forecasting tournament, the Good Judgment Project found that some people are consistently better at assigning realistic probabilities, and its approach outperformed intelligence analysts with access to classified data. [18] The group treats a post-mortem on every forecast as a core step. [19] NHL teams review decisions informally, but there is little public evidence that any team runs a disciplined, written prediction-and-review system.

**What would be new.** Treating forecast accuracy as a measurable organizational skill, and applying it to every major hockey decision rather than to a few analytics projects.

**Objections and answers.**

| Objection | Design answer |
| --- | --- |
| "This will be used to blame people." | Reviews focus on the process and the reasoning, not the person. Good decisions can have bad outcomes. |
| "Hockey decisions aren't probability questions." | Most are. "Will he be a top-four defenseman by 24?" has an answer. Writing down a confidence level is simply honesty about uncertainty. |
| "It's extra paperwork." | One short form per major decision. The ledger covers dozens of decisions a year, not thousands. |

**Rough cost (estimate).** One to two staff, plus simple software. Roughly $150,000 to $400,000 per year.

**First twelve months.**

- Build the prediction form and review calendar.
- Log every major decision made during the season.
- Complete the first calibration audit on a sample of past draft classes.

**How success is measured.** Calibration improves over time; the organization can name specific beliefs it changed because of the ledger.

**Kill criteria.** If after two seasons the ledger has not changed a single scouting method, model or decision process, it is being filled out but not used, and it should be redesigned or stopped.

### Initiative 2: The Toledo and Grand Rapids Proving Ground

**In one sentence:** new practice methods, development approaches and tactical ideas are tested in Toledo first, then in Grand Rapids, before they are trusted in Detroit.

**What it is.** Automakers do not put an untested engine into a production car. They run it at a proving ground first. This initiative gives Detroit the same discipline, and it treats the Toledo Walleye (ECHL), the Grand Rapids Griffins (AHL) and the Red Wings as one connected development relationship rather than three separate teams. Promising findings from research, the Fellowship or the coaching staff start in Toledo, where the stakes are lowest and testing can be most aggressive. What survives moves to Grand Rapids for a tougher test against stronger competition. Only what survives both is promoted to the NHL roster.

The proving ground is part of the operating system rather than a standalone project. It is the step between "this looks promising" and "we are betting the season on it."

**What gets tested.**

- Practice structures and drill design
- Individual development interventions, such as skating or scanning work
- Tactical systems, including the Concepts Over Positions pilot (Initiative 4)
- Recovery and workload approaches, with players' consent

**Why Detroit.** The link already exists. Shawn Horcoff has been general manager of the Grand Rapids Griffins since February 2022, alongside his role as assistant GM, and was named interim GM in September 2026. [52] A leadership group that knows the affiliates this well is well placed to use them deliberately.

**What already exists.** Using the minors for development is universal. Using them as a deliberate testing environment is less common. In baseball, Sig Mejdal spent most of two seasons with Houston's minor league affiliates, sometimes as a uniformed coach, teaching players and coaches how to use data before becoming an assistant GM in Baltimore. [20] That is the translation work this initiative is built around.

**A new lever.** Under an agreement the NHL and CHL finalized in August 2026, CHL players drafted in the first round become eligible for assignment to the AHL at 19, starting this season. [21] That means Detroit's top junior prospects can now develop inside the Proving Ground a year earlier than before.

**Why Toledo goes first.** The ECHL affiliate should be the most aggressive test environment in the system. Roster stakes are lower, so Toledo can try ideas earlier and more often, and its coaches become a real part of the development relationship rather than an afterthought. Grand Rapids then confirms what works against a higher level of competition before anything reaches Detroit.

**Building it into the affiliation.** Detroit does not own the Walleye. The team is owned by Toledo Arena Sports Inc., an Ohio not-for-profit corporation, and its staff runs a lean operation: in 2019, the head coach also handled much of the front office work. [53] The current affiliation agreement with Detroit runs through the 2026-27 season. [54] That makes this season's renewal the natural moment to write the Proving Ground into the relationship: shared development goals, a Detroit-funded development coach or test coordinator based in Toledo, and agreed limits that protect the Walleye's ability to compete for its own fans.

**Objections and answers.**

| Objection | Design answer |
| --- | --- |
| "Detroit doesn't control Toledo." | True today. The affiliation is up for renewal after this season, so the testing terms and the extra staff Detroit would fund go into the new agreement. |
| "Minor league players aren't lab rats." | Only coaching and development methods are tested, never anything that reduces a player's opportunity, ice time or health. |
| "Toledo and Grand Rapids still need to win." | Tests are scoped and scheduled with each affiliate's coaching staff, who help design them. |
| "Sample sizes are too small." | Some tests will be inconclusive. Those results are recorded, not hidden, and can be repeated across both affiliates. |

**Rough cost (estimate).** Mostly staff time: a dedicated test coordinator and analyst support. Roughly $200,000 to $500,000 per year.

**First twelve months.**

- Agree a testing charter with the Toledo and Grand Rapids coaching staffs and players.
- Run two or three small, clearly scoped tests.
- Report results, including failures, to hockey operations at season's end.

**How success is measured.** Methods tested in Toledo and Grand Rapids are adopted in Detroit, and methods that failed there never reached the NHL roster.

**Kill criteria.** If affiliate coaches or players consistently experience the program as disruptive, or no tested method is adopted in two seasons, it should be redesigned.

### Initiative 3: The Detroit Scouting Network

**In one sentence:** an open hockey forecasting competition that identifies the sharpest evaluators anywhere, and turns the best of them into paid contributors to Detroit's scouting.

**What it is.** Most organizations measure scouts by reputation and experience. This initiative measures evaluators by accuracy. Participants make specific, scored predictions about prospects and players: Will this player be drafted? Will he play 100 NHL games? Will this defenseman become a top-four regular? Predictions are scored against outcomes over several seasons.

The structure has three tiers:

1. **Open competition.** Anyone can enter a public forecasting contest built on public information, with prizes for accuracy.
2. **Contributor tier.** Consistently accurate forecasters are offered small paid contracts to file reports on assigned players and leagues.
3. **Pipeline.** The very best contributors become candidates for part-time or full-time scouting roles.

**Why Detroit.** European scouting was built on finding what others missed. [12] This initiative applies the same principle to people: finding the evaluators other teams have not noticed.

**What already exists.** The Good Judgment Project gathered thousands of online volunteers, identified the top performers, and found that roughly 70 percent of those top forecasters kept their status from one year to the next. [22] Its best forecasters outperformed intelligence analysts with classified data. [18] In hockey, Stathletes' Big Data Cup already uses an open competition to identify emerging analytics talent. [24] No NHL team is publicly known to run a scored, open forecasting network for player evaluation.

**What would be new.** Measuring evaluation skill directly, at scale, and building a scouting pipeline from the results.

**A legal constraint that shapes the design.** Federal labor rules do not allow people to volunteer their services to for-profit private employers. [23] That is why this network is a competition feeding paid contractor roles, not a volunteer scouting corps. *[Structure to be reviewed by employment counsel.]*

**Guardrails.**

- Participants never represent the Red Wings or contact players, families, coaches or agents.
- Participants see only public information, never internal lists or data.
- Nobody pays for the right to scout. Any paid education offered separately stays separate from the competition.

**Objections and answers.**

| Objection | Design answer |
| --- | --- |
| "Amateurs can't out-scout professionals." | Some can on specific questions, and scoring reveals who. Professional scouts can enter too. |
| "It will create noise." | Only calibrated forecasters' input is weighted. Everyone else stays in the competition tier. |
| "It will threaten our scouts." | It is a tool for scouts, and a hiring pipeline, not a replacement for them. |

**Rough cost (estimate).** Platform, prizes, contractor fees and one coordinator. Roughly $250,000 to $750,000 per year.

**First twelve months.**

- Obtain legal review and design the scoring system.
- Launch a pilot competition on one draft class.
- Identify the first cohort of contributors from early results.

**How success is measured.** Top forecasters' accuracy holds over multiple years, and their input improves Detroit's own calibration.

**Kill criteria.** If after three draft cycles no contributor beats a simple benchmark, such as consensus draft rankings, the network is not adding knowledge and should end.

### Initiative 4: Concepts Over Positions

**In one sentence:** build a system around situational roles and shared concepts rather than fixed positions, then acquire the best hockey players instead of filling positional slots.

**What it is.** Traditional hockey assigns every player a fixed label: left wing, center, right wing, left defense, right defense. This initiative asks what happens if roles are defined by situation instead. Every skater learns a shared set of concepts: how to defend when caught low, how to support a breakout, how to occupy space in the offensive zone. Players still have primary responsibilities, but they are expected to read and fill any role the play demands.

This is not "no structure." It is structure built on concepts that every player understands, rather than positions only some players are trained for.

**Why Detroit.** Scotty Bowman used the Russian Five as a five-man unit built on puck possession, and only selectively, because he worried opponents would figure out how they did it. He also said Sergei Fedorov "could have been an all-star defenseman," and moved him back to defense during an injury stretch. [25] Detroit has already won with players who did not fit neatly into one box.

**What already exists.** Hockey is drifting this way: activated defensemen, rotating forechecks and four-forward power plays all loosen positional lines. Other sports offer precedents, including Dutch "total football" in soccer and the positionless era in basketball. What does not appear to exist publicly is an NHL organization that builds its roster strategy, development curriculum and tactical system around this idea deliberately.

**What would be new.** Two things:

1. **A development curriculum** that teaches universal competencies from Toledo upward.
2. **A roster hypothesis to test:** if the market pays a premium for positional need, Detroit may be able to acquire better overall players at lower cost by caring less about the label. That is a hypothesis for the Decision Ledger to test, not a claim.

**Where the idea has limits.**

- **Faceoffs** still reward centers.
- **Handedness** matters for zone entries, one-timers and breakouts.
- **Line changes** require clear responsibilities, or the defensive zone empties.
- **Specialized defensive skills**, like gap control and net-front battles, take years to build.
- **Contracts:** positional comparables shape arbitration and negotiations, and agents may resist a system that blurs a player's category.

**Objections and answers.**

| Objection | Design answer |
| --- | --- |
| "Positionless means chaos." | The system keeps clear situational responsibilities. It removes rigid labels, not structure. |
| "Coaches won't buy in." | It starts as a coach-designed pilot in Toledo, not a mandate. |
| "Players will lose value." | Versatility tends to add value. The initiative tracks this through the Player Experience Office. |

**Rough cost (estimate).** Mainly coaching and video staff time. Under $250,000 per year.

**First twelve months.**

- Define the core concepts with coaches.
- Pilot selected concepts in Toledo and Grand Rapids practices and specific game situations.
- Test the roster hypothesis against historical contract and performance data.

**How success is measured.** Pilot teams maintain or improve performance while players demonstrate broader competencies; the roster hypothesis is confirmed or rejected with evidence.

**Kill criteria.** If the pilot measurably hurts results over a full minor league season without offsetting development gains, it should return to the drawing board. The pilot expands to Detroit only on evidence.

### Initiative 5: The Goalie Bullpen

**In one sentence:** stop searching for one "true number one" goalie and build a deliberate group of viable goaltenders instead, developed and deployed like a bullpen.

**What it is.** A philosophy change and a development system for the position that swings more games than any other. Instead of building around a single starter and a backup, Detroit keeps several viable goaltenders and uses them on purpose:

- **Carry three when it makes sense.** The NHL roster can carry a third goalie in place of an extra skater who rarely plays.
- **An on-deck starter.** The Grand Rapids starter is treated as on deck. When an NHL goalie is cold or hurt, the on-deck goalie gets real starts, not just emergency duty.
- **A Toledo-to-Detroit pipeline.** Young goalies play every night in Toledo and Grand Rapids, where they get the game reps they need, and move up as they earn it.
- **One goaltending department.** A director of goaltending owns how Detroit identifies, drafts, develops, acquires and coaches goalies at every level, so the philosophy is the same from the ECHL to the NHL.

**Why it makes sense.** Goaltending is the least predictable thing in hockey. A 2026 study of 550 consecutive-season pairs over 18 seasons found that the part of save percentage a goalie personally controls barely carries over from one season to the next, a correlation of about 0.14. The effect of team defense carries over far more, at about 0.63. [56] Paying a premium for a "true number one" means paying the most for the least predictable asset on the roster. A bullpen spreads that risk the way a pitching staff does.

**Why Detroit.** The pipeline already exists in this report: Toledo and Grand Rapids are where the Proving Ground lives. Waiver rules also favor goalies. A goaltender who signs his first contract at 18 can stay waiver exempt for six seasons or 80 NHL games, compared with five seasons for a skater who signs at the same age. [57] That gives Detroit years in which a young Grand Rapids starter can come up for starts without the risk of losing him on waivers.

**What already exists.**

- **Calgary** created a Director of Goaltending role in December 2020 to build the best process to "identify, draft, develop, procure and coach goalies" across the organization. [58] Dustin Wolf, a seventh-round pick in 2019, came through that system. [59]
- **Carolina** carried three goaltenders for much of the 2025-26 season. Brandon Bussi, the least proven of the three, led the team in starts and wins and relieved Frederik Andersen in the Stanley Cup Final. [55]
- **The debate is live.** Buffalo's general manager said early last season that "the three-goalie thing doesn't work." By season's end, his coach said it had. Seattle opened the season planning to use three goalies all year. [55]

**What would be new.** Calgary built the department. Carolina showed the deployment can win a Cup. Detroit's version would combine both on purpose, write the on-deck role into how Toledo and Grand Rapids are run, and let goaltending research from the Hockey Science Institute (Initiative 10) inform every decision about who plays.

**Objections and answers.**

| Objection | Design answer |
| --- | --- |
| "Every Cup team needs a true number one." | Carolina just won with three. And the data says a starter's results are far less predictable than his contract assumes. |
| "A third goalie in the press box gets rusty." | Detroit only carries three when all three are genuinely in the rotation. Development goalies play every night in Toledo or Grand Rapids. |
| "Goalies want defined roles." | Rotation and recall rules are set and explained before the season, so every goalie knows how starts are earned. |
| "It costs a roster spot." | It replaces an extra skater who rarely plays, and it protects against losing a goalie on waivers. |

**Rough cost (estimate).** A director of goaltending and an added development goalie coach: roughly $300,000 to $700,000 per year in hockey operations spending. A third NHL goalie's salary counts against the salary cap like any other player's, so the bullpen is a roster-construction choice as much as a spending one.

**First twelve months.**

- Name a director of goaltending to own the philosophy at every level.
- Set written rotation and recall rules for Detroit, Grand Rapids and Toledo.
- Map which goalies in the system are waiver exempt, and plan each one's path.

**How success is measured.** Team goaltending holds up across a full season even when individual goalies slump; fewer games are lost to a cold or injured starter; young goalies reach the NHL ready, without being exposed to waivers.

**Kill criteria.** If after two seasons the rotation produces worse goaltending than a conventional starter-and-backup model, or development goalies stall for lack of reps, return to a conventional structure and keep only the department.

### Initiative 6: The Player Experience Office

**In one sentence:** treat the full experience of being a Red Wing, for players and their families, as a competitive advantage that is designed, measured and improved.

**What it is.** A small, dedicated office responsible for everything about a player's life with the organization that is not directly hockey:

- **Family support:** relocation, housing, schools, spouses' and partners' integration into the community.
- **Career planning:** education, financial literacy and preparation for life after hockey.
- **Transitions:** support for players who are traded, sent down, injured or retiring.
- **Honest feedback:** confidential annual surveys and exit interviews with every departing player, reviewed by senior leadership.

**Why Detroit.** Wojnowski described the leadership search as looking for someone who can handle an NHL where players have more power than ever. [1] In that environment, the teams players want to join, and stay with, have a real advantage in free agency, extensions and trade approvals. Detroit's history, city and ownership stability are strong starting points. This office makes that advantage deliberate.

**What already exists.** Many NHL teams employ player engagement or player development staff who support players off the ice. What is less common is treating player experience as something measured with data and reported to leadership, the way a scouting department reports on prospects.

**What would be new.** Measurement and accountability: a clear picture, updated every year, of how players and their families experience the organization, and a record of what changed because of it.

**Objections and answers.**

| Objection | Design answer |
| --- | --- |
| "Players won't be honest." | Surveys are confidential and administered independently. Exit interviews happen after a player has already left. |
| "This is soft stuff." | Retention, free-agent interest and willingness to sign extensions are hard, valuable outcomes. |
| "Every team already does this." | Many teams provide support. Few measure it and act on the results systematically. |

**Rough cost (estimate).** Two to four staff plus program costs. Roughly $400,000 to $900,000 per year.

**First twelve months.**

- Hire a director with player-relations experience.
- Run the first confidential survey of current players and families.
- Begin exit interviews with every departing player.

**How success is measured.** Improving survey scores over time; more players choosing to stay or sign; stronger free-agent interest in Detroit.

**Kill criteria.** If players do not engage with the surveys after two seasons, or leadership does not act on the findings, the office is not doing its job and should be restructured.

### Initiative 7: The Detroit Hockey Fellowship

**In one sentence:** every year, bring a small group of accomplished people from outside hockey to Detroit and ask them to tell us something about the game hockey people have not figured out.

**What it is.** An annual cohort of three to five senior visiting researchers, appointed for one to two years. They come from fields that study movement, decisions, systems or performance: professional soccer, Formula 1, automotive engineering, aerospace, robotics, neuroscience, motor learning, decision science, baseball player development. Each Fellow is paired with hockey people and given access to data, video, staff and a research question.

**The first cohort: soccer.** Hockey and soccer share a deep structure. Both are continuous-flow invasion sports built on space, possession, transitions, pressure and off-puck or off-ball movement. The chair of the world's largest hockey analytics conference has said soccer is the sport that has come furthest in data-driven analysis. [31] The first cohort should be anchored by a senior tracking-data researcher from elite professional soccer, deliberately chosen without a hockey analytics background.

The value is not only the models a soccer researcher would bring. It is their instinct for where those models stop working. Hockey has things soccer does not: boards that act like an extra teammate, stick reach that extends a player's control, skaters who glide instead of cutting, line changes every shift and a puck that leaves the ice. A researcher who has watched space-control models struggle at the edges of a soccer field is well placed to test them against the edges of a hockey rink.

**Why Detroit.** This is the left-wing lock in modern form: someone going outside the league to find ideas the NHL does not yet have. [14] Ilitch has also said he sees merit in bringing in experience and skills that may not exist inside the organization today. [1]

**What already exists.**

- **Pittsburgh** runs a paid Hockey Research and Development Fellowship of 6 to 12 months for candidates with backgrounds in math, physics, statistics, computer science and engineering, with a path to full-time roles. [26]
- **Cross-sport hiring is established.** Pittsburgh hired its director of hockey systems after 15 years in baseball operations technology with the San Diego Padres. [27] Carolina's championship GM came from chemistry and nanotechnology research. [6]
- **Soccer ideas have already reached public hockey research.** Soccer's Expected Threat model has been adapted to hockey, [28] and a 2026 paper combined it with a rink-control model. [29]

**What would be new.** Pittsburgh's program is a pipeline for early-career quantitative talent. Detroit's would bring in senior people from entirely different fields, on purpose, every year. It turns outside thinking from an occasional hire into a permanent process.

**Objections and answers.**

| Objection | Design answer |
| --- | --- |
| "Outsiders don't understand hockey." | That is the point. They are paired with scouts, coaches and analysts who do. |
| "Fellows will leave and take ideas with them." | Fellowship agreements protect proprietary work; the knowledge stays in the question bank and the ledger. |
| "Top soccer analysts won't come." | A defined term, meaningful resources and a genuinely new problem are attractive. The pitch is research freedom, not a job title. |

**Rough cost (estimate).** Three to five Fellows plus research support. Roughly $750,000 to $2 million per year.

**First twelve months.**

- Define the first cohort's research questions with hockey operations.
- Recruit the soccer-anchored first cohort.
- Hold a structured mid-year and end-of-year review of findings.

**How success is measured.** Each cohort produces at least one finding adopted by scouting, coaching or development, and at least one question added to the question bank.

**Kill criteria.** If two consecutive cohorts produce nothing adopted and nothing new worth studying, the model is not working.

### Initiative 8: The Player Data Partnership

**In one sentence:** become the organization players trust most with their data, by using it to help them, not to negotiate against them.

**What it is.** A voluntary research partnership in which players who opt in share wearable and performance data in exchange for real value: personalized recovery insights, injury-risk research, career longevity studies and long-term health information. A strict, written firewall prevents any research data from being used in contract negotiations, trades or salary arbitration.

**Why Detroit.** Ilitch named injury prevention and sports science as priorities beyond analytics. [1] Detroit also has a nearby partner in the University of Michigan Concussion Center, which conducts multidisciplinary research on concussion prevention, diagnosis and outcomes. [34] A trusted data partnership would make that kind of collaboration possible.

**What already exists, and the constraint.** Player data is already governed by agreements:

- The NHLPA says wearable use is completely voluntary, and players can access their data and ask their club to delete it at any time. The wearables agreement sits outside the CBA and is not public. [32]
- The NBA's CBA bars wearable data from being used in contract negotiations, trades or waivers, and MLB's makes wearables voluntary with strict confidentiality rules. [33]

Most teams treat these rules as a wall. This initiative treats them as a foundation.

**What would be new.** A reputation. Any team can buy the same devices. What cannot be bought quickly is years of players seeing that their data was used only in their interest. That trust becomes a recruiting advantage: Detroit as the place where careers last longer.

**Objections and answers.**

| Objection | Design answer |
| --- | --- |
| "The NHLPA won't allow it." | The partnership is built with the union's input and exceeds existing protections. Participation is voluntary. |
| "Management will be tempted to use the data." | A written firewall, independent oversight and severe consequences for breaches. |
| "Players won't opt in." | They will if they receive clear, personal value, and if early participants vouch for it. |

**Rough cost (estimate).** Sports science staff, secure data systems, partner research and independent oversight. Roughly $500,000 to $1.5 million per year.

**First twelve months.**

- Consult the NHLPA and player representatives on design.
- Write and publish the firewall policy to players.
- Launch a small voluntary pilot focused on recovery and injury prevention.

**How success is measured.** Rising voluntary participation year over year; findings that measurably reduce injuries or improve recovery; players citing the program positively.

**Kill criteria.** Any breach of the research-to-contract firewall ends the program immediately. If participation stays below a meaningful share of the roster after two seasons, it should be redesigned.

### Initiative 9: The Innovation Partner Program

**In one sentence:** co-fund and strengthen Detroit's hockey research through genuine research partnerships with Michigan universities and companies, not logo sponsorships.

**What it is.** A structured program that invites a small number of partners in automotive engineering, materials, technology, health care and higher education to work on hockey research alongside the Red Wings. Partners contribute funding, equipment, expertise or facilities. In return they get real research problems, access to elite athletes under strict rules, and the chance to apply findings in their own fields.

**Why Detroit.** This is where the Detroit engineering argument becomes concrete.

- **Wayne State University** developed the Wayne State Tolerance Curve for head injury in 1960. According to the university, that work remains a foundation for motor vehicle and sports equipment safety standards. [17] Its injury biomechanics program says it has built on its automotive relationships to expand into sports. [35]
- **The University of Michigan's** performance research lab has collaborated with the university's athletic department, professional sports organizations and industry partners on wearable technology research, including a study with Samsung. [36]

Few NHL markets have this combination of automotive engineering heritage, injury science and research universities within driving distance of the arena.

**What already exists.** Industry and university partnerships in sports science are common, as the Samsung and University of Michigan study shows. [36] What is less common is an NHL team organizing those partnerships around its own long-term research agenda.

**What would be new.** Partners are chosen for the research they make possible, not the exposure they buy, and the program is explicitly designed to reduce ownership's net cost for the Institute and its conference.

**Objections and answers.**

| Objection | Design answer |
| --- | --- |
| "Partners will want our data or marketing claims." | Contracts separate proprietary hockey research from shared research, and limit marketing use. |
| "Universities move slowly." | Projects are scoped with clear timelines; slow partners are not renewed. |
| "This is just sponsorship with a new name." | Every partnership must produce a defined research output, reviewed annually. |

**Rough cost (estimate).** One to two staff to manage partnerships and contracts, roughly $200,000 to $400,000 per year. The goal is for partner contributions to exceed that cost many times over.

**First twelve months.**

- Hold exploratory conversations with Wayne State University and the University of Michigan.
- Agree on one pilot research project with each.
- Define standard partnership terms that protect proprietary work.

**How success is measured.** Partner contributions offset a growing share of research costs; joint projects produce findings Detroit uses.

**Kill criteria.** If partnerships generate publicity but no usable research within two years, the program should be narrowed to the partners that deliver.

### Initiative 10: The Detroit Hockey Science Institute

**In one sentence:** a dedicated research institution whose mission is to find the important questions about hockey nobody has answered, study them seriously, and turn what it learns into Red Wings knowledge.

**What it is.** Not another arena, not a practice facility and not an analytics office with a new name. It is a hockey laboratory: a place where scouts, coaches, data scientists, biomechanists, motor-learning researchers, engineers and video analysts work together on long-term questions.

Its mission is deliberately different from day-to-day hockey operations. Operations asks how to make tonight's lineup better. The Institute asks what Detroit does not yet understand about the game.

**The flagship question: how do hockey players learn, and why do some skills transfer while others don't?** This area is chosen because it is mostly invisible from outside. Rivals can copy a forecheck from video. They cannot watch how Detroit develops its players. Almost every important research question connects to it:

| Research question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| What is hockey sense, and can parts of it be trained? | Turns the most valued and least understood trait into something measurable |
| How do elite players scan before receiving the puck? | Soccer research by Geir Jordet has used eye-tracking with elite players in real matches, a foundation hockey can build on [30] |
| Why do dominant junior players struggle to transfer to the NHL? | Improves draft projection and development plans |
| How much can skating mechanics change after 18? | Shapes development investment in older prospects |
| What happens to skating and decisions late in a long shift? | Informs shift length, deployment and conditioning |
| Is traditional practice structured for how players actually learn? | Could improve every practice in the organization |
| Which attributes survive aging? | Informs extensions and long-term contracts |
| What about goaltending is still poorly measured? | Feeds the Goalie Bullpen (Initiative 5) |

**How it works.**

- **Project teams, not silos.** A question like "why do highly drafted defensemen fail?" gets a team: a scout, a data scientist, a biomechanist, a development coach, a motor-learning researcher and a video analyst.
- **Honest grading.** Every finding is labeled proven, promising or not measurable yet. An 18-month project that concludes "we can't reliably measure this" is still knowledge, archived and revisited as technology improves.
- **Direct links to the loop.** Findings feed the Decision Ledger and are tested in the Toledo and Grand Rapids Proving Ground before they change NHL decisions.

**Why Detroit.** Two of the region's research institutions offer specific expertise. Wayne State's injury biomechanics labs use high-speed cameras and Vicon motion tracking, and have worked with industry partners for decades. [35] The University of Michigan's performance research lab studies movement biomechanics, wearable-sensor performance metrics, and how motor control changes with age and skill. [37] That is the Institute's research agenda almost word for word.

**What already exists.** Pittsburgh has a named Hockey Research and Development department, including a VP of hockey research, development and strategy and a director of hockey systems. [27] A 2023 survey found a typical NHL team employs about three analytics staffers. [2] Some of the most established on-ice skating biomechanics research comes from universities, such as the University of Calgary's work measuring skating technique on the ice. [38] What does not appear to exist is a dedicated, multidisciplinary hockey research institution run by an NHL organization.

**What would be new.** Scale, mission and mix: a long-term research agenda, a physical laboratory with experimental ice, and non-analytics scientists working as equals alongside data specialists.

**Built in phases, funded by proof.**

| Phase | Timing | What it includes | Capital (estimate) | Annual operating (estimate) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Research unit | Years 1 to 3 | 8 to 12 staff, university partner labs, equipment, existing ice | About $2M to $6M | About $2M to $4M |
| 2. Lab building | Years 3 to 5 | Dedicated 15,000 to 25,000 sq ft lab facility near existing ice | About $12M to $25M | About $4M to $8M |
| 3. Full Institute | Years 5 to 10 | Adds a dedicated, instrumented research ice surface | About $25M to $50M total | About $6M to $12M |

**Where the cost estimates come from.** A traditionally designed single-sheet rink is priced at about $15 million to $20 million with Midwest pricing. [39] A two-sheet facility is estimated at $37.6 million to $46 million. [40] Dry lab and computational space often runs $150 to $300 per square foot, [41] while life sciences lab fit-outs averaged $741 per square foot in 2026. [42] A biomechanics lab sits between those benchmarks. Specialized research buildings can cost far more: a 20,000-square-foot research and imaging center opened in 2025 for $31 million. [43] The ranges above are wide on purpose.

**Objections and answers.**

| Objection | Design answer |
| --- | --- |
| "It becomes an expensive boondoggle." | No building until phase 1 produces findings hockey operations actually uses. |
| "It collects data without producing knowledge." | Every project starts with a question and ends with a graded finding. No question, no project. |
| "It becomes a rival power center." | It reports to the President of Hockey Operations under a written charter, with protected capacity for long-horizon work. |
| "Coaches won't trust lab findings." | Coaches sit on project teams, and findings are tested in Toledo and Grand Rapids before reaching them. |

**Folded in:** contract and aging research that feeds the Decision Ledger, and goaltending research that supports the Goalie Bullpen (Initiative 5).

**The public arm: the Detroit Hockey Science Conference.** Once phase 1 has real research to show, the Institute hosts an annual gathering in Detroit for the people who study how hockey players move, learn, get injured and improve: biomechanists, motor-learning researchers, sports scientists, equipment engineers, coaches and graduate students. Detroit does not need to employ every brilliant person studying hockey. It benefits if those people want to come through Detroit.

- **Two kinds of research.** Proprietary research that gives Detroit an edge stays internal. Public research that surrenders nothing is presented, building Detroit's reputation and attracting future Fellows and partners.
- **Science, not analytics.** The analytics lane is crowded. The Colorado Avalanche hosted the 2026 HALO conference for NHL analytics departments, [48] Linköping University hosts LINHAC, [49] Ottawa hosts a hockey analytics conference, [50] and Stathletes runs the Big Data Cup. [24] Biomechanics, motor learning, injury prevention and practice design are far less crowded, and they match Detroit's research strengths.
- **Start small.** An invitation-only research symposium in years 3 to 5, with a graduate student research competition. At full scale, roughly $500,000 to $1.5 million per year (estimate), partly offset by registrations and partners. If three editions bring little serious research or recruiting benefit, it returns to the smaller symposium model.

**First twelve months.**

- Hire a research director who can work with both scientists and hockey people.
- Build the first question bank with scouts and coaches.
- Launch two or three phase 1 projects with university partners.

**How success is measured.** Findings adopted by scouting, coaching and development; questions formally answered or retired; the calibration and cycle-time measures from Part III.

**Kill criteria.** Phase 2 is not funded unless phase 1 produces adopted findings. If phase 1 produces none in three years, the unit should be restructured rather than expanded.

### Initiative 11: Detroit Red Wings Centers of Excellence

**In one sentence:** a long-term network of development and coaching education centers in key hockey markets, run under the Red Wings name, that serves players and coaches from every program without competing against any of them.

**What it is.** Centers of Excellence would offer skill and skating development, strength and conditioning, nutrition, education, life skills and, above all, coach education. Over time, they could exist in strategically important markets such as Michigan, Ontario, Sweden, Finland, Czechia and emerging hockey regions.

Three principles define them:

1. **A Center never ices a team.** The moment it competes for players, it becomes a club, and a club can shrink goodwill as easily as build it.
2. **Players never become Red Wings property.** No one attending a Center gains or loses anything in the NHL Draft, and every NHL team can draft them. Players must be 18 by September 15 of their draft year to be eligible at all. [47]
3. **Coaches are the core audience.** Educating coaches spreads better development to thousands of players, and lets Detroit learn from coaching cultures around the world.

**Why Detroit.** The long-term payoff is indirect: relationships and goodwill. Imagine hundreds of players, coaches, trainers, future scouts and future executives around the hockey world who can say the Red Wings invested in them before Detroit ever needed anything from them. That does not guarantee anything. It does build a network no rival can copy quickly.

**What already exists.** The NBA runs academies offering year-round development, education, health and life-skills programming, with full scholarships for participants. By 2023, more than 90 NBA Academy participants had committed to or attended NCAA Division I schools, and several graduates had reached the NBA. [44]

**The cautionary tale.** In 2020, ESPN reported that coaches at NBA academies in China had complained of physical abuse of young players and a lack of promised schooling at partner facilities run by local authorities, and the Xinjiang academy was closed. [45] The NBA later acknowledged it needed more direct oversight and the ability to make staffing changes. [46] Any Detroit program must learn from that.

**What would be new.** An NHL team building an international development and coaching network as a learning system, not a talent pipeline, with safeguarding and research ethics built in from the start.

**Design requirements.**

- Partner with existing clubs, federations and universities, but only with direct oversight, safeguarding standards and staffing authority.
- No research data collected on minors in the first phase.
- Full compliance with privacy laws in each country, including European rules on health and biometric data. *[Legal review required.]*
- Respect for national federations, which may see an NHL brand as a threat unless it clearly adds value.
- **Full transparency and alignment** with the NHL, the NHLPA, the collective bargaining agreement and the IIHF before any center opens, and at every step after.

**Objections and answers.**

| Objection | Design answer |
| --- | --- |
| "This circumvents the draft." | It cannot. Centers create no rights to any player, and are open to scouts from every team. |
| "European federations will resist." | Lead with coach education and partnership, where the value to them is obvious. |
| "If it works, the league will ban it." | Design it to be league-friendly from day one: open, transparent and non-exclusive. |
| "The payoff is too slow." | That is why it is a long bet, funded only after earlier initiatives prove the model. |

**Rough cost (estimate).** Highly variable. A single partner center could cost roughly $1.5 million to $4 million per year; a mature network could reach $5 million to $15 million per year.

**First twelve months (once funded, in years 3 to 5).**

- Launch one coach-education partnership in Michigan or Ontario.
- Complete legal and safeguarding reviews for one international market.
- Define how coaching knowledge flows back into the Institute's question bank.

**How success is measured.** Coaches trained, partner satisfaction, development ideas imported into Detroit's own programs, and long-run relationships across the hockey world.

**Kill criteria.** Any safeguarding failure triggers immediate independent review. If a pilot center produces no knowledge Detroit uses and no measurable goodwill within three years, the network should not expand.

## Part V: How It Fits Together

### One system, not eleven projects

The initiatives are not a menu. Each one feeds a stage of the same learning loop, and each gets stronger because the others exist.

Following the loop:

1. **Bring knowledge in.** The Fellowship imports ideas from other fields. The Scouting Network finds sharper evaluators. Later, Centers of Excellence widen the network of coaches and development ideas Detroit learns from.
2. **Create new knowledge.** The Institute turns questions into graded findings, with help from the Innovation Partner Program and the Player Data Partnership.
3. **Test it.** Promising findings, including Concepts Over Positions, are tried in Toledo, then Grand Rapids.
4. **Decide.** Hockey operations uses what survived testing, alongside the judgment of its scouts and coaches.
5. **Learn from results.** The Decision Ledger compares predictions with outcomes and sends new questions back to the start.

The Player Experience Office and the Institute's Hockey Science Conference support the whole loop: one keeps players and families invested in the organization, and the other keeps talented researchers wanting to come to Detroit.

### Governance: who owns this

A research program that sits outside hockey operations becomes a rival power center. One that is buried inside it gets raided for short-term work. The design avoids both.

- **It reports to the President of Hockey Operations**, consistent with Ilitch's commitment to give that leader full authority. [1]
- **A written charter protects long-horizon research.** A fixed share of the Institute's capacity is reserved for questions that will not pay off this season.
- **Coaches and scouts sit on project teams**, so findings are built with the people who will use them.
- **Ownership receives an annual learning report**: the five measures from Part III, what changed, and what was shut down.

### The ten-year roadmap

The sequence is deliberate. Cheap, fast initiatives go first and produce evidence. Expensive initiatives wait until that evidence exists.

| Phase | Timing | Initiatives launched | What must be proven before moving on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Start the loop** | Year 1 | Decision Ledger, Toledo and Grand Rapids Proving Ground, Scouting Network pilot, Concepts Over Positions pilot, Goalie Bullpen, Player Experience Office | Decisions are being logged and reviewed; at least one tested method is adopted |
| **Build capacity** | Years 1 to 3 | Detroit Hockey Fellowship, Player Data Partnership, Innovation Partner Program, Institute phase 1 | Findings adopted by scouting, coaching or development; committed research partners |
| **Invest** | Years 3 to 5 | Institute phase 2 lab building; first Center of Excellence pilot; first research symposium | Phase 1 has produced measurable value; partner funding is in place |
| **Scale** | Years 5 to 10 | Institute phase 3 research ice; Centers of Excellence network; full Hockey Science Conference | Clear, documented return from every earlier phase |

### What year one looks like

The first season requires no new building and relatively little money. It is mostly a change in how decisions are made and reviewed.

- Every major decision is logged with a written prediction.
- A calibration audit of past draft classes begins.
- Toledo and Grand Rapids run two or three small, agreed tests, and the renewed Toledo affiliation includes Proving Ground terms.
- The Scouting Network pilot, the Goalie Bullpen and the Player Experience Office launch.
- Recruitment begins for the first Fellowship cohort and the Institute's research director.

### Questions worth asking every leadership candidate

Because the next President of Hockey Operations will set the plan, these ideas are most useful as questions:

1. How would you know, three years from now, whether the organization had become smarter?
2. How do you record decisions so the organization can learn from the ones that went wrong?
3. Where would you look for ideas that the other 31 teams are not looking at yet?
4. How would you use Toledo and Grand Rapids to test ideas before betting an NHL season on them?
5. What would you want to understand about hockey that nobody understands today?

A strong candidate will have their own answers. The point of this report is not to supply them, but to make sure the questions get asked.

## Part VI: Costs, Risks and When to Stop

### What it would cost

*Every figure below is the author's estimate, built from the initiative ranges in Part IV. They are for discussion, not budgeting.*

| Stage | Annual operating cost | Capital |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Year 1** (Ledger, Proving Ground, Scouting Network, Concepts Over Positions, Goalie Bullpen, Player Experience Office) | About $1.4M to $3.5M | Minimal |
| **Years 1 to 3** (adds Fellowship, Player Data Partnership, Innovation Partner Program, Institute phase 1) | About $4.8M to $11.4M | About $2M to $6M |
| **Years 3 to 5** (adds Institute lab building, first Center pilot, symposium) | Rising toward mature levels | About $12M to $25M for the lab building |
| **Mature program, year 10** (all eleven initiatives) | About $14M to $36M | About $27M to $56M cumulative, mostly the Institute |

**How to read these numbers.**

- **Most of the early cost is people, not buildings.** Year one is affordable for almost any organization. The large capital decisions come later, and only if earlier work proves itself.
- **The Centers of Excellence and the full Institute drive the high end.** Without them, the mature program costs far less.
- **Some cost is offset.** The Innovation Partner Program is designed to bring in funding, equipment and expertise, and the Institute's conference generates registrations and partners.
- **None of it counts against the salary cap.** The NHL cap limits player payroll, which is $104 million per team in 2026-27. [51] Hockey operations spending is not capped. [3] At maturity, the full program would cost roughly the equivalent of 13 to 35 percent of the 2026-27 cap each year, but entirely outside it, and it builds knowledge that lasts for decades rather than a single contract term.

### The biggest risks

| Risk | Why it matters | How the design responds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Research that changes nothing** | The most common failure of analytics and research groups | Every project starts with a decision it could change; adoption is measured |
| **A rival power center** | Research that competes with hockey operations gets ignored or resented | Reports to the President of Hockey Operations under a charter |
| **Player and union trust breaks** | Misused data would damage recruiting and relationships | A written firewall; any breach ends the Player Data Partnership |
| **Harm involving minors** | One failure could damage the organization's reputation | Safeguarding standards, direct oversight, no research data on minors in phase 1 |
| **League rule changes** | Leagues can limit spending, as the CFL did with football operations in 2019 [4] | Invest in durable knowledge, habits and partnerships, not headcount alone |
| **Rivals copy the ideas** | Visible advantages erode quickly | Focus on work that is hard to see from outside: development, projection, decision-making |
| **Cost creep** | Buildings and staff tend to grow | Phased funding; no capital without proof |
| **Legal exposure** | Volunteer labor, privacy and international law are complex | Counsel review before launch of the Scouting Network, Player Data Partnership and Centers |

### Kill criteria at a glance

Every initiative has a defined point at which it should be redesigned or stopped. Committing to these in advance is what separates a disciplined program from an expensive hobby.

| Initiative | Redesign or stop if... |
| --- | --- |
| Decision Ledger | Two seasons pass without it changing a method, model or decision |
| Toledo and Grand Rapids Proving Ground | No tested method is adopted in two seasons, or players and coaches find it disruptive |
| Detroit Scouting Network | After three draft cycles, no contributor beats consensus rankings |
| Concepts Over Positions | The pilot hurts results over a full minor league season without development gains |
| Goalie Bullpen | Two seasons of worse goaltending than a starter-and-backup model, or development goalies stall |
| Player Experience Office | Players do not engage, or leadership does not act on findings, after two seasons |
| Detroit Hockey Fellowship | Two consecutive cohorts produce nothing adopted and nothing worth studying |
| Player Data Partnership | Any firewall breach; or participation stays low after two seasons |
| Innovation Partner Program | Two years of publicity without usable research |
| Hockey Science Institute | Phase 1 produces no adopted findings in three years; no phase 2 without proof. The conference returns to a symposium after three weak editions |
| Centers of Excellence | Any safeguarding failure triggers review; no expansion without value in three years |

### What this report does not know

Intellectual honesty matters more than confidence here. The biggest uncertainties are:

- **Real costs.** Equipment, staffing and construction estimates need professional quotes.
- **Legal structure.** The Scouting Network, Player Data Partnership and Centers of Excellence all require legal review.
- **Internal practice.** An outside author cannot see what the Red Wings already do. Some of these ideas may already exist inside the organization in some form.
- **Adoption.** The hardest part of any learning organization is not research. It is getting people to change their minds. No design fully solves that; leadership does.

## Conclusion: Go Looking Again

When Chris Ilitch talked about innovation in September, he did not point to a single trade or a single signing. He pointed to a pattern. A coach who saw how three players could fit together. An organization willing to draft players who might never be allowed to leave their country. An assistant coach who went to Sweden looking for a better way to defend. A scouting staff that kept finding stars where other teams were not watching.

None of those advantages lasted forever, and none of them could have. Every one was eventually copied. What made Detroit great was not any single discovery. It was the habit of discovery.

This report has tried to turn that habit into something an organization can actually build: a loop that asks better questions, tests ideas honestly in Toledo and Grand Rapids, writes down what it expects and learns from what actually happens. Some of the eleven initiatives could start this season for roughly what a depth player costs. Others are long bets that should only be funded once the earlier work proves itself. Every one of them comes with a point at which it should be stopped.

The Red Wings do not need to recreate 1989 or 1997. The frontiers those teams explored have been mapped. But there are still questions about how hockey players learn, decide, age and improve that nobody has fully answered. Some organization is going to start answering them first.

Detroit has done this before. The next leader of Red Wings hockey operations has a chance to make it the way the organization works again.
