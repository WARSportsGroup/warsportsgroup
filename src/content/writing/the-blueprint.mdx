Detroit is looking for a person, maybe two. They also need a system.

That distinction is the whole ballgame. Since Steve Yzerman transitioned out of the general manager's chair on July 15, the conversation around this franchise has been a name-generation exercise. Horcoff or Draper. Dellow or Ventura. Internal or external. Old school or new school. Meanwhile the actual problem sits untouched: this organization has spent a decade running in a modern NHL with a thin, under-resourced, consensus-driven hockey operations department, and no single hire fixes that. You do not solve an infrastructure problem with a personality.

So here is the argument. Detroit should not hire only a general manager. Detroit should hire a President of Hockey Operations and a General Manager, in that order, as two distinct jobs with two distinct mandates, bound together by a written charter that says exactly who decides what.

Not because the franchise needs an old-school hockey guy to babysit an analytics guy. An analytically inclined General Manager certainly does not need a chaperone. The real case for two chairs is the span of control. One executive cannot simultaneously own the five-year capital plan, the ownership relationship, the department's headcount and infrastructure, *and* work the phones, negotiate every contract, run the draft table, manage the head coach, and answer for a 10-year playoff drought at every press conference. Yzerman tried to do both and he failed. The department stayed thin. That is not a coincidence, and it will happen again to whoever is next if the job description does not change.

Call it the Architect (PoHO) and the Operator (GM). The Architect owns the system: identity, structure, resourcing, timeline, and the relationship with Chris Ilitch. The Operator owns the roster: transactions, negotiations, the draft table, the coaching staff, the day-to-day. They overlap by design at exactly one point, which is player acquisition strategy, and the charter dictates how disagreements there get resolved.

Two-headed front offices fail more often than they succeed, and they fail for one reason: nobody writes down who breaks a tie or how conflict gets resolved. Toronto's version drifted for years before ownership had to choose. Vegas built a genuinely successful version because George McPhee and Kelly McCrimmon defined the handoff before they needed it. The structure is not the risk. Ambiguity is the risk.

What follows is a first-season blueprint. Not a wish list of trades. A sequence.

## Establish Organizational Identity

We have been saying for years that Detroit has an identity problem, and it is obvious to see if you watch as many of their games as I do.

Every organization in this league will tell you it values compete, character, and skill. Let me be blunt: that is not an identity, that is a poster. An identity is a written, testable definition that a 24-year-old amateur scout watching a U18 game in Sweden and a Grand Rapids assistant coach running a Tuesday practice can independently apply to the same player and reach the same conclusion.

Three artifacts, due before the season opens:

**1. The Game Model.** One page. How the Red Wings intend to play, expressed in terms that can be measured. No generic "we want to be hard to play against" filler. Something closer to: we deny controlled zone entries at a top-10 rate, we generate a defined share of our offense off the forecheck, we hold a positive expected goals share in tied-game states. Five to eight markers. Public inside the facility and tracked on a weekly basis.

**2. The Player Archetype Rubric.** The Game Model translated into evaluation language. If the model demands defensemen who exit under pressure, that becomes a graded axis on every scouting report at every level. It wouldn’t matter if it is applied to a 17-year-old in the OHL or a 29-year-old UFA. Same axes, same scale, same file format. This aids in ceasing the practice of being drawn to one specific type of player. You have your game defined, now you know which player archetypes you need and when.

**3. The Development Standard.** Detroit, Grand Rapids, and Toledo run the same system, the same terminology, and the same skill priorities. A prospect promoted in December should not be learning a new vocabulary. Right now the honest question nobody has answered publicly is whether the Griffins are developing players for the Red Wings' system or for the Griffins' record.

The test is simple and brutal. Put the AHL head coach, an amateur scout, and an NHL assistant in three separate rooms and ask each to write three sentences describing what a Red Wing is. If the answers do not match, you have a brand, not an identity.

One warning. The identity cannot be nostalgia, we have tried that and watched it fail. "Hockeytown" is an asset in the marketing department and a liability in hockey operations. The 1997 Red Wings are not a template, they are a memory, and a decade of trying to feel like that team instead of building the next one is part of how Detroit got here.

## Audit the Entire Hockey Ops Department

You must audit the entire department within the first 100 days. The stakes are too high to not do so and it must be done as meticulously as an individual or team could possibly do. All remaining staff must be given a “Hockey Operations MRI” and it must be determined how useful or replaceable these individuals are.

What the audit actually covers:

**Headcount against the league median.** Department by department: amateur scouting, pro scouting, hockey analytics, player development, sports science, video. I have argued before that Detroit needs a Director of Pro Scouting, a Director of Hockey Analytics, and a Director of Player Development as defined, empowered roles rather than duties absorbed into an assistant GM's portfolio. Nothing that has happened since has changed my mind. The audit's job is to convert that argument into a budget request with numbers attached for Chris Ilitch.

**The decision autopsy.** Pull the last five drafts, the last twenty trades, and the last three free agency classes. For each of those decisions by the hockey club, find out: who recommended it, who dissented, what was the written thesis, and what happened. The point is not to assign blame to people who are leaving anyway. The point is to find out whether dissent existed at all. If the file shows unanimous rooms for a decade, that is the finding, and it is a bigger finding than any individual miss. The days of “Detroit Hockey group think” must be exterminated.

**The data infrastructure audit.** Reporting suggests analytics in Detroit has functioned in an advisory capacity rather than a decision-making one, which again leads me to believe there has been nowhere near the amount of teamwork necessary over the Yzerman era in the Wings front office (which is laughable and sad and annoying all at once). Advisory though is really just a polite word for optional. The audit needs to establish what data is collected, who owns it, whether it lives in a warehouse or in somebody's spreadsheets, and whether a regional scout can actually query it from a rink in Kitchener. A team can hire a brilliant analytics director and still get nothing if that person spends year one building pipelines that should have existed in 2018.

**Reporting lines.** Who does Todd McLellan report to, how often, etc? Who does Dan Watson report to, how often, etc? Who do the pro scouts report to and how often? How many times do they meet? How often do the AGMs meet with the GM and what do they discuss regularly? Does player development have authority over a player's summer, or does it have suggestions? This stuff is incredibly easy to figure out and outline in the building. This is a billion dollar organization and people need to stop running it like it is their country club clubhouse.

Deliverable, due December 1: a proposed org chart with named roles, weekly protocols, hockey operations headcount and cost, delivered to ownership as a single document. Not a conversation. A document, a shared vision, in stone.

## Define the Competitive Window

This is the pillar where the new regime has to tell the truth, first internally and then, in a modified form, publicly.

Look at the cap sheet, because the cap sheet has already made this decision.

For 2026-27, Detroit carries roughly $84.4 million against a $104 million ceiling, leaving about $19.6 million in space with Simon Edvinsson still unsigned. That is a mid-table commitment on a mid-table roster that finished 41-31-10 and missed the playoffs for the tenth straight year after holding a playoff position deep into the season.

Now look at the year after. For 2027-28, Detroit has roughly $50.3 million committed against a projected $113.5 million cap. Call it $63 million in space. Alex DeBrincat, Andrew Copp, Mason Appleton, Justin Faulk, John Gibson, and Daniil Tarasov all come off the books at once. The cap is projected to reach $123 million in 2028-29.

That is not a cap sheet. That is a loaded weapon.

Which produces the single most important strategic conclusion in this entire blueprint: **Year 1 is not about 2026-27 at all. It is about being ready for the Summer of 2027.**

The long-term core is already committed. Dylan Larkin (still a Red Wing) at $8.7 million through 2031. Moritz Seider at $8.55 million through 2031. Lucas Raymond at $8.075 million through 2032. Edvinsson is pending, and he will be signed. There are reports too that DeBrincat is willing to remain in Detroit past his current contract. The prospect clock, meanwhile, runs through Axel Sandin Pellikka, Carter Bear, Nate Danielson, Trey Augustine, Michael Brandsegg-Nygård and still somewhat also Marco Kasper and Emmitt Finnie. Are those players ready to help you be a playoff team this year? Most likely not. But what about in 27-28 or 28-29? I feel much better about that trajectory and where a lot of this personnel may be.

So the honest window declaration reads like this: Detroit is not tanking and Detroit is not contending in 2026-27. Detroit is spending this season building the balance sheet, the evidence base, and the department that will allow it to strike in the summer of 2027, with the intent of being a real playoff team in 2027-28 and hopefully a threat in 2028-29. By the time we get to 2030, Detroit should be positioned as a strong and contending team in the Eastern Conference.

## Player Decisions

Sequence matters more than content here. Three tiers, in order.

### Tier 1: Sign Edvinsson. Now. Before the hire, if possible.

Edvinsson remains an unsigned restricted free agent in August, offer-sheet eligible, with the club reportedly prepared to match anything. Detroit is not going to lose him (most likely not; but nothing is seemingly impossible this offseason). That is not the problem. The problem is what it signals to a 23-year-old top-pair defenseman who has been sitting in limbo since July 1. This trend of making your top drafted players sweat out their second contract negotiations needs to cease and desist. You won’t trade this player in a package for Quinn Hughes in late 2025 (according to reports) but you also won’t get him locked up ASAP when the window opens? Make it make sense.

He should be locked up on a long term deal. The cap math argues for it independent of any projection about the player: against a cap moving from $104 million to $113.5 million to $123 million, a fixed AAV in the mid-eight-figures becomes a shrinking percentage of the ceiling every single year. A deal in the 8.5-9 million dollar range is roughly 8.2 percent of today's cap and under 7 percent by 2028-29. The term is the discount.

This does not require trade authority. It requires a signature. There is zero reason why this can’t be done by now.

### Tier 2: Resolve Larkin, on the new regime's terms.

Dylan Larkin requested a trade shortly after the season ended on April 15 and has not moved off it according to reports. That request is now four months old and it was made under a front office that no longer exists, that is worth remembering.

That last point deserves more weight than it is getting. Did Larkin ask out of Detroit? Or did he ask out of a specific version of Detroit, one built on a decade of losing and a leadership structure he had apparently stopped believing in. The single variable that has actually changed since April is that structure.

So the first move is not a trade call, it is a meeting. An authentic one with open dialogue from all involved parties. The Architect and the Operator sit down with Larkin and show him the 2027 plan, in writing, with dates. Not a pep talk. The document. If the plan is real, it is the only argument that has any chance of working, and it is an argument nobody was in a position to make in April obviously since Yzerman was still around and in control of Detroit’s immediate hockey future, but today that is not the case any longer.

If he still wants out, then move him, and move him on a structure that serves the window rather than the news cycle. The return has to be denominated in 2027 and 2028 currency: young NHL-ready players, high picks, and cap-clean contracts. Not a rental package that helps a contender and buys Detroit a headline about decisiveness.

Two things to be clear-eyed about. A 30-year-old top-line center at $8.7 million with five years remaining is a depreciating asset, and his highest-value trade windows are this fall and the 2027 deadline, not 2028. And accepting a discounted return to end “the drama” is the single most expensive mistake available to this regime in year one.

### Tier 3: The 2027 expiring class becomes deadline inventory

DeBrincat is the marquee decision. Forty-one goals, 85 points, age 28, expiring at $7.875 million. There are exactly two acceptable outcomes: extend him by December at a number that fits the 2027 structure, or trade him at the deadline for assets that do. Letting him reach July 1 and walk for nothing would be an unforced error that Detroit has, historically, been fully capable of committing as we all unfortunately know. The good news with DeBrincat is it appears as if Detroit is simply where he wants to be. I would be more than okay paying DeBrincat his fair market value and having him be your veteran sniper in the NHL as you get this train back on its tracks and heading towards a mutually agreed upon destination.

Smaller stakes and different logic on Copp, Appleton, Faulk, and Gibson. Every one of them is either an extension that fits the plan or a deadline asset. There is no third category. Most are likely the latter with maybe Copp being an exception.

### The HC question

Todd McLellan did not create this. New regimes generally want their own bench, and that instinct is not wrong on principle, but the sequencing is. The team must define the Game Model first, then evaluate whether the coach can execute it. Firing a coach for failing to run a system that was never written down teaches the organization nothing and costs it a year. The report is that McLellan has 26-27 and 27-28 left on his deal. I could see a world where Todd and the team part ways in the Summer of 2027 if this new regime wants their own bench bosses. Nonetheless, I have said at length I like and respect Coach McLellan and I think he can do a fine job moving forward, but at the same time there are a lot of exciting young up and coming coaches in the NCAA and Major Junior who could be ready for a jump to the NHL soon.

## Rebuild Trust

Four audiences, four different trust problems. Each audience must be dealt with compassionately, with accountability, and with deep reverence for what they bring to the table of the Detroit Red Wings hockey family.

**The Room.** Larkin is the symptom, not the disease. When a captain asks out, the safe assumption is that others have privately considered it. The repair mechanism is not a speech, it is a pattern: roles defined in writing in September, promises made in September delivered by December, and the captain's situation resolved with dignity in either direction. Players do not need to be told the truth is coming. They need one instance of it arriving on schedule.

**The Fanbase.** The currency here is not optimism, it is specificity. Detroit fans have been sold patience by the last two General Managers and have nothing to show for it, so another appeal to patience will be (correctly) ignored by me and hundreds of thousands of other Wing-Nuts. What we have never been given is a plan and a set of conditions. Say the year. Say what has to be true for it to happen. Say what you will do if it does not. A quarterly public state-of-the-franchise from the President of Hockey Operations, on a schedule, would be the single biggest departure from the last decade's communication posture. Fans forgive slow if you are authentic and accountable. They do not forgive vague and generic spin (which is what we have gotten from Detroit Hockey Operations since 2019).

**The League.** This one is underrated and it has real dollar value. Detroit's reputation among agents and rival executives directly determines what happens in July 2027, when the plan says the franchise finally has money and needs people to take its call. A franchise-defining RFA sitting unsigned in August and a captain in limbo since April are both visible to every agent in the sport. Closing those two files quickly and cleanly is not just roster management, it is reputation repair with the exact audience that matters most for the 2027 strike. The way the Detroit Red Wings “do business” so to speak needs to drastically change and once it does the rest of the league will see it, note it, and react to it.

**Ownership.** The uncomfortable one, and the one this franchise's last regime never resolved publicly. A hockey operations department cannot out-execute its own resourcing. The Architect's first deliverable to Chris Ilitch is the audit document: headcount, infrastructure, development staffing, cost, and the specific competitive consequence of underfunding each line. Ilitch either signs it or does not. If he signs it, the internal trust rebuild has a foundation. If he does not, everything else in this blueprint is decoration, and the new regime will be having the same conversation in 2029 that the last one had in 2025. Chris has to hire the right person(s) and sign off on their vision and support it (and not from a distance, be a little more involved).

## Build a Sustainable and Successful Hockey Ops Methodology

Everything above is a first-year plan. This pillar is what makes it survive the first-year plan and into year two and beyond.

**The Decision Rights Charter.** A cornerstone Hockey Ops document. Written in week one, before there is a disagreement to resolve. It specifies what the President decides alone, what the General Manager decides alone, what requires both, and what escalates to ownership. It names the tiebreaker explicitly, by role, in advance. This document is the entire reason a two-chair structure works or does not, and it takes a day to write.

**The Written-Thesis Rule.** Every acquisition above a defined threshold gets a one-page memo *before* it happens. The org's thesis, comparable players, expected role on the team, and what would falsify or hinder the role. Filed within 72 hours. Reviewed at 12, 24, and 36 months. This is the cheapest high-leverage change available to any front office in hockey, and it is the mechanism that turns a decade of decisions into a decade of learning. Protocols and procedures like this or adjacent to this are how you learn and gain hockey wisdom before it is way too late.

**Structured Dissent.** A designated challenger in every draft and major trade meeting, rotated, whose explicit job is to argue the other side. If Detroit's decision autopsy comes back showing unanimous rooms, this is the fix. Consensus that costs nothing is worth nothing. If you want more collaboration and teamwork, then all pieces of this Hockey Ops team need to be comfortable challenging ideas and sharing new ones.

**Analytics With a Vote.** The Director of Hockey Analytics reports to the General Manager, sits in the room by right rather than by invitation, and owns defined authority over a specific decision class. Contract term and AAV modeling is the natural one. The failure mode is not having smart analysts, it is having smart analysts whose work is consulted after the decision has been made. Detroit’s previous regime had it backwards or simply didn’t care. Analytics need a say, just like the eye test needs a say, just like years of hockey experience needs a say. The Team over me.

**One Evaluation Language.** Amateur, pro, and development all grade on the identity rubric, so a player's file is continuous from his draft year through his NHL debut, and perhaps extends to his first few years in the league too. When a prospect or young player stalls, the organization can see whether the miss was in the evaluation or in the development, which is a distinction Detroit has had no way of drawing. All of your amateur and professional scouts should speak the same language, be fluent in it, and be able to break any player down uniformly to their director or any other member of the Detroit Hockey Ops staff.

**Director of Player Development Overhaul.** This is where most organizations, including this one, quietly underinvest. The Player Dev tier of your Hockey Operation cannot by any means be structured in an anemic way. Skill and player development is not only a coaching question, it is a question of ice allocation, summer programming, and individual development plans that have a named owner and a date attached to them. Every prospect gets a written plan with three measurable priorities and a quarterly review. If nobody owns it, it does not happen, and the player development arm in Detroit needs way more detailed people running it with more modern development practices studied, adopted, and executed.

**Audit becomes Annual.** The audit is not an event. Run it every April/May. An organization that audits itself on a schedule does not need a crisis to change. When you have a team of people who are truly obsessed with the process, with development, and growth: they do these kinds of things on their own because it is purposeful and of the utmost importance. When you have an “Old Boys Club” running the team, why audit anything? Those days must be dead and gone.

## Conclusion

Detroit's problem was never a shortage of hockey knowledge. The building has been full of it for thirty years. Eleven Cups worth of it sits in the front office and the rafters.

The problem is that hockey knowledge was always only part of the equation, not the whole formula. With that knowledge you need lots of other things: detailed processes, hiring people based on merit and not connection, being able to understand and process league data and trends, obsessively communicating with your team and other teams, etc.

Ken Holland was the “right” person. Steve Yzerman was the “right” person. Both of them are Hall of Fame hockey minds and both of them were handed a job that has outgrown what any single hockey mind can do. So do not hire the right person. Build the right structure, then fill it.

Two chairs, an Architect and an Operator. One Charter that says who decides what. An identity written down where the whole organization can be graded against it. An audit that produces a budget and an org chart instead of a press release. A window declared out loud. A cap sheet protected for the summer of 2027. A methodology that makes the organization smarter every year whether or not any individual decision works out.

That is the blueprint. It is not flashy, and it will not produce a parade in 2027. But the fruit it could bear in the not-too-distant future is worth the patience it asks for. This decision is of the highest importance. This team simply cannot afford to get it wrong again. Ten years into this drought, exciting is not what this franchise needs. It needs an authentic and detailed plan that the whole team can be held to.
