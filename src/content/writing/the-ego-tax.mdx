Every hockey organization budgets its money. Almost none budget for ego, and ego spends anyway.

I know because I've paid that tax myself. But before we get to my bill, let's talk about the one every organization is running up.

## Everything Is Asking for a Piece of You

Running a hockey team means there is always too much to pay attention to. Rosters, recruiting, schedules, staff, parents, agents, budgets, travel, injuries, the next game, the next season. Everything is asking for a piece of you, and there isn't enough of you to go around.

Every organization works with the same four finite resources: time, money, people, and attention. And every leader is responsible for protecting three things at once: the health of the organization, the well-being of the players, and winning as often as possible. Over the long run, those three feed each other. Healthy organizations develop well-cared-for players, and those players win. In the short run, they collide constantly. The star who broke team rules. The veteran playing hurt in a playoff push. The prospect you're tempted to rush.

So how do you decide what gets your finite resources when everything cannot matter equally? My answer is process. Not "process" the way it gets thrown around in postgame press conferences. I mean something specific:

**Process is how an organization decides where its resources go.**

Good process spends resources on what's true. Bad process spends them on whatever is loudest, most urgent, or most flattering to the people in charge. And the most expensive force working against good process never shows up on a budget sheet.

## Results Can Lie

The standings are the scoreboard everyone watches, which is exactly why they're dangerous. Winning can be bought on credit. You can overplay your top guys, trade away your future, and lean on older players while your young ones stall. The standings look great. The organization is quietly getting weaker. And the bill always comes.

It works the other way too. Talent can cover for bad process for a while. A team can be full of people you'd never want near your young players and still post a great record.

Colorado learned this early in Joe Sakic's tenure. In his first season in charge of hockey operations, the Avalanche put up 112 points, then collapsed in the first round, blowing a 3-2 series lead against Minnesota and losing Game 7 in overtime at home. It looked like the start of something. It wasn't.

Tampa Bay lived an even louder version. The 2018-19 Lightning tied the NHL record with 62 wins and piled up 128 points, then became the first Presidents' Trophy winner ever swept in the first round.

Neither result told the truth. The 112-point season hid real problems. The sweep hid a championship core. That's the point: if you allocate resources based on the last result, you'll be wrong in both directions.

## The Ego Tax

Here's a number every hockey executive knows: 32 teams, one Stanley Cup a year. Most general managers will never win one. Every one of them can get fired.

That creates an incentive problem, and I want to be clear that it's a system problem, not a character flaw. When you're evaluated on whether you keep your job, the rational move is often to protect it. Chase the playoff bubble instead of committing to a real rebuild. Trade picks for a deadline rental. Keep playing the player you signed to a big contract so nobody asks why you signed him.

Pride protects the decision. Fear protects the job. Either way, the organization pays.

That's the ego tax: ice time, cap space, roster spots, and staff attention spent defending decisions instead of making good ones.

Colorado paid it in full. In 2016-17 the Avalanche went 22-56-4, and their 48 points left them last in the league by a wide margin, all while scraping the salary cap ceiling. What makes the story worth studying is what came next. Sakic acknowledged that he'd realized, probably a year too late, that he had too many older players taking up too much cap space. That's a GM naming his own tax out loud. Most never do.

The ego tax isn't just a front-office problem. I see it in scouting, and I've caught it in myself. Scouting for a Tier 2 junior program is a finite-resource job in its purest form. There are more players, games, and rinks than any one person can cover. The only way to spend that time well is to scout against your organization's actual gaps: knowing the current roster, its limitations, and what's leaving. You can't do that if ego won't let you admit where your roster is thin.

Then there's the subtler version. By November, I might really like a player on a top 18U AAA team. Do I go watch him a fourth or fifth time, or cover a team I haven't seen enough of? That fifth look feels like diligence. A lot of the time it's confirmation. I'm spending attention to feel sure, not to learn something new. Every extra look at a player I already like is a first look I didn't take somewhere else, and I don't know what I'm missing.

My rule now: every extra viewing has to answer a specific question. "Does he defend the same way against a top line?" earns another look. "I just want to see him again" doesn't. If I can't name the question, I go cover new ground.

There's also conversion risk. You can pour time into a player who picks somewhere else or never had real interest. You can't eliminate that. You can only operate with it in mind, which is one more reason not to over-invest in confirmation.

## Priorities Are a Calendar, Not a Ranking

Most talk about priorities treats them like a ranked list. In practice, what matters most changes by the week.

In my operations work, launching a season is a peak. Everything is execution, and there's no room for anything else. A couple of months later, the work changes completely. That's when you gather feedback, start early conversations about next season, get plans on people's radars, and evaluate your own processes. If you're smart with the lows, you set yourself up for the next high.

Scouting runs on the same rhythm, on two axes at once. The first is when in the season. Early, you stack views, see teams as often as you can, and catch practices when possible. By the end of the calendar year you should have a main list, and the work shifts from breadth to depth: digging into players as people and building the relationship between organization and prospect.

The second axis is how far out you're looking. Watching 18U surfaces players who may need a home next season. Watching 16U surfaces players one to three years out. High school hockey feeds both. The heaviest diligence goes toward next year's team, because that's where roster turnover is coming.

Every hockey organization lives on a calendar of peaks: draft, free agency, training camp, trade deadline, playoffs. Those windows are loud, urgent, and visible. The stretches between them are quiet, and quiet is where the long-horizon work lives.

Survival-mode organizations react to the peaks. Process-driven organizations win the valleys.

And the most important thing built in the valleys never shows up on a depth chart.

## Relationships Are a Resource

"People" as a resource usually means headcount: how many coaches, scouts, analysts. But the real asset isn't the bodies. It's the trust between them.

Trust takes time to build, it can be spent, and it's expensive to earn back. That makes it a resource like any other, and the valleys are where you build it. You can't learn how a player communicates during a disciplinary meeting. That knowledge has to exist before the crisis. Valley time builds relationship capital. Peak moments spend it.

I learned this coaching. More than once, one of my best players broke team rules, and all three commitments landed on the table at once. Winning: I needed him in the lineup. Organizational health: rules only mean something if they apply to everyone. His well-being: letting it slide teaches him the wrong lesson, and his teammates are watching what I do.

Here's what I've landed on. I walk into that meeting with the consequence already decided. That's the process part. It means the conversation can be about the player instead of about me figuring out what to do in the moment.

But the delivery can't be one-size-fits-all. Ruling every situation with the same iron fist doesn't work anymore. Today's player wants to be somewhere the person in charge genuinely cares about him as a person, not just as a player. That isn't special treatment. Same standard, different conversation. The standard doesn't bend. The delivery adapts to the person.

The check against favoritism is simple. Call it the fourth-liner test: would the fourth-liner get the same standard and the same care? If yes, it's leadership. If no, it's favoritism.

To make that work, I prepare every high-stakes conversation on three levels, whether it's discipline, a draft meeting, or anything else:

- What needs to be communicated
- How to communicate it to this specific person
- How it lands: they leave held to the standard, respected, and knowing they're genuinely cared about

They may not like the outcome. They should never doubt the standard or the care.

Tampa's front office has said the same thing in its own way. When Julien BriseBois extended Jon Cooper, he pointed to Cooper's ability to build impactful relationships with everyone from players to staff as a trademark of his tenure. That's a GM naming relationship capital as a reason to invest.

## Humility Is the Audit Function

If ego is the tax, humility is the audit that catches it.

I don't mean humility as a personality trait. I mean it as a working function. Your outlook is a resource too, because it decides where your attention goes. A leader who does not challenge his biases regularly spends his attention confirming what he already believes. A curious one spends it finding what he's missing. Same hours, same staff, same budget, completely different return.

Hockey has plenty of people who think they know everything, and a lot of the time it doesn't work out the way they envisioned. Meanwhile, research and information have never been more accessible. The barrier to getting smarter isn't access. It's willingness. Curiosity alone isn't enough, because you can't audit your own blind spots by yourself. That's what a strong staff is for. In the quiet stretches there has to be open, regular dialogue, including the uncomfortable conversations. Those aren't optional. You don't get better by doing the same things over and over.

Now, my bill.

One season, one of my players was the top-scoring center in the league and didn't make the all-league team. The spot went to a goalie I thought was average. I was so frustrated that I logged off the league call. It didn't look good, and I had real repair work to do afterward: explaining where I was coming from and showing people my approach had shifted.

My intent was right. I was advocating for a player who had earned recognition. My delivery spent credibility I'd need later, cost me time repairing relationships, and derailed momentum in other areas. The player didn't gain anything from it either. Right intent, wrong delivery, and I paid for both.

Sometimes in hockey, things just happen. If you take them personally instead of letting them roll off, they cost you in places you didn't expect.

Tampa's GM showed the opposite response on a much bigger stage. After the sweep, BriseBois said he wouldn't overreact, kept his confidence in Cooper, and admitted he couldn't pin the loss on any one thing, even though a clean narrative would have been easier for everyone. Ownership kept things status quo, and BriseBois kept the group together. Tampa went on to win back-to-back Stanley Cups in 2020 and 2021 and reached the Final again in 2022.

Years later, explaining why he still backed Cooper, BriseBois described him as a coach who never gets too high or too low, which lets him weather the storms. That's the lesson I learned on a league call, practiced at the highest level.

## The Honest Limit

Process doesn't guarantee outcomes. It improves your odds.

Tampa's patience produced two Cups and three straight Finals. It has also produced a run of early exits. This past spring brought a fourth straight first-round loss, this time to Montreal, and BriseBois still made clear Cooper is staying. Patience has costs too, and whether this stretch vindicates it is a fair debate.

Colorado's patience paid off faster. After the 48-point season, the Avalanche could have fired Sakic, or Sakic could have fired Jared Bednar and traded core pieces. Instead, they stayed patient. When Matt Duchene wanted out, Sakic waited for the best possible offer, and that deal brought back Sam Girard and the first-round pick that became Bowen Byram. The next season, Colorado finished with 95 points and made the playoffs. After repeated playoff exits and questions about Bednar in big moments, Sakic stuck with his coach again. They won the Cup in 2022.

I'm not claiming to know what happened inside either building. What the public record shows is two organizations that refused to let the last result spend their resources for them. I'd rather bet on that than on survival mode. Ego and fear can preserve the appearance of stability while quietly making the organization weaker.

## The Operator's Toolkit

Principles only matter if they change what you do on Monday. Four tools:

1. **Build a calendar map.** Before the season, chart your peaks and valleys. Assign specific long-horizon work to each valley in advance, so the quiet months don't get swallowed by admin.

2. **Run a valley review.** Use a quiet stretch to audit last season's decisions honestly, especially the ones you most want to defend. That's where the ego tax hides.

3. **Make every extra look answer a question.** A fifth viewing, another meeting, another report: name what you're trying to learn. If you can't, spend the time somewhere new.

4. **Prepare What, How, and Lands.** Before every high-stakes conversation: what needs to be said, how to say it to this person, and how it should land.

Time, money, people, and attention will always be finite. Everything will never matter equally. The organizations that win over time aren't the ones that avoid hard trade-offs. They're the ones that decide them honestly, before the pressure hits, with people humble enough to keep checking their own work.

Every organization budgets its money. Ego spends anyway. The only question is whether you're auditing it.
